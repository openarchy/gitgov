### **The school that rehearsed the future**

#### **Article 14 of 21 of the Governance as Versioning series**

 _This is Article 14. The thinker is John Dewey. The central Git concept: the full cycle of issue → branch → review → merge or revert as democratic pedagogy in action. This article is transversal: it does not anchor to a single step in the 21 Steps—it justifies the entire process as lived democratic practice._

* * *

#### What you’ll find here

Have you ever been part of an organization where people had the right to vote, but nobody knew how to propose anything, question anything, or change anything? Where democracy existed in the bylaws, but every decision landed in the laps of two or three people who knew how the system worked?

Dewey knew that problem. And he located the cause in a place nobody expected: the school.

* * *

## **1\. Where it all begins**

Chicago, autumn of 1896. In a modest building near the university, a woman in her early thirties asks a group of children to build a miniature farm. It’s not a game. The children have to measure the land (mathematics), research how farms worked a hundred years before (history), test which materials hold weight (science), and decide together who does what (politics). The woman is Alice Dewey, wife of the philosopher who conceived that place. The place is the Laboratory School of the University of Chicago. And the philosopher is John Dewey, a 37-year-old man trying to prove a dangerous thesis: democracy is not learned from books. It is learned by doing.

The school existed for eight years. Children did not sit in rows listening to a teacher dictate. They worked on real projects, discussed how to solve problems, and voted on collective decisions about their own daily routine. Whoever made a mistake tried again. Whoever disagreed argued.

In the same Chicago of 1896, three kilometers away, factories employed ten-year-old children for twelve hours a day. Public schools ran like assembly lines: the teacher commanded, the student obeyed, and whoever asked too many questions was punished. American democracy was proud of its free elections. Dewey looked at all of that and saw a farce. The most democratic country in the world was training its citizens for obedience.

Thirty years later, Dewey would publish the most devastating response Walter Lippmann’s pessimism ever received.

* * *

## **2\. Historical context**

America at the start of the twentieth century lived a contradiction. It had a constitution, elections, and separation of powers. But the daily life of people was pure hierarchy. In the factory, the owner commanded. In the school, the teacher dictated. In the community, everyone minded their own business. You practiced democracy one day every four years, on election day. The other 1,460 days? Obedience.

In 1922, Lippmann published _Public Opinion_ and concluded that ordinary citizens are incapable of governing. His solution: let experts decide; the people merely choose between elites. Dewey read it and disagreed with the force of someone who had spent his life building the alternative. In 1927, he responded with _The Public and Its Problems_. The thesis: the problem is not that citizens are incapable. The problem is that nobody taught them to participate.

* * *

## **3\. The central idea**

Democracy is not a system of government. It is a way of life.

The sentence sounds vague. It isn’t. Dewey meant something concrete: real democracy demands constant participation in every decision that affects you. It isn’t voting every four years and going home. It’s discussing, proposing, deciding together, making mistakes, correcting, and learning. Every day.

Think about learning to swim. Nobody learns to swim by reading a swimming manual. You get in the water, swallow water, try again, and discover the movement. Swimming is a practical skill. Democracy is too. You don’t become a democratic citizen by attending a lecture about democracy. You become one by practicing: discussing real problems with real people, making decisions with real consequences, and taking responsibility when you make mistakes.

Before Dewey, the dominant view treated democracy as an electoral mechanism. After him, it became hard to ignore that a country can have perfect elections and zero democracy if the entire life of its people is hierarchical. A school that trains obedience does not produce active citizens. A factory that forbids questioning does not produce participatory workers. A community where nobody knows anyone does not produce collective deliberation.

Dewey identified the vicious cycle: society says it is democratic but creates no conditions for people to learn to live that way. And then blames people for not participating.

* * *

## **4\. Developments**

### **The eclipse of the public**

Dewey had a concept that explains much of what we see today. He called it the “eclipse of the public.” The idea: in small communities (the Vermont village where he was born, for instance), everyone knows each other, knows who does what, and discusses face-to-face. The “public” exists because people see each other.

In modern metropolises, the public disappeared. You live in a building and don’t know your neighbor. Decisions that affect you are made in places you’ve never been, by people you’ve never seen. The public exists (millions are affected by the same decisions), but it does not recognize itself as a public. Fragmented, invisible to itself.

Dewey did not propose going back to the village. He proposed creating new forms of community adapted to modern scales. The school would be the laboratory: the place where families meet, children practice deliberation, and adults participate in real decisions about education.

### **Learning by doing, governing by doing**

Dewey’s method at the Laboratory School inverted everything. Instead of memorizing dates, children investigated problems. Instead of obeying imposed rules, they negotiated collective rules. Instead of receiving a grade from above, they evaluated their own work alongside their peers.

Dewey called this “experimental method applied to social life.” The logic is the same as science: you have a problem, you form a hypothesis, you test it, you evaluate the result, you adjust. It’s no different from what a repository does: someone opens an issue (problem), proposes a solution (branch), tests it, submits it for evaluation (review), incorporates or discards it (merge or revert). Governing, for Dewey, is experimenting collectively.

The irony is that governments love to talk about democracy but rarely want truly active citizens. The active citizen questions, criticizes, proposes, and causes discomfort. The passive citizen votes and stays silent. The educational system of most countries continues to produce the second type.

### **The answer to Lippmann**

The divergence between Dewey and Lippmann is one of the most productive in the history of political thought. Lippmann said, “The public is ignorant; it doesn’t understand complex problems; it needs expert guidance.” Dewey replied, “The public is not ignorant by nature — it is ignorant by design.”   
If you strip from a person every opportunity to participate, decide, make mistakes, and learn, that person will appear incapable. But the incapacity is not the person’s: it is the system’s, which trained them for passivity.

Lippmann’s solution (government by experts) generates the very problem Lippmann described (apathetic public). Dewey saw the circularity: the less people participate, the less they know how to participate. The less they know, the more someone concludes they need guidance. More guidance, less participation. The loop closes.

## **5\. Historical legacy**

Dewey influenced progressive education worldwide. Montessori schools, project-based pedagogy, active learning: all carry Dewey’s DNA. In Brazil, Anísio Teixeira (1900–1971) brought his ideas and founded experimental schools that transformed public education in the 1930s. The Escola Parque in Salvador, inaugurated in 1950, applied Dewey at municipal scale: children learned by doing, participated in decisions, lived democracy in their daily lives.

Brazil’s 1988 Constitution, in defining education as preparation for active citizenship, echoes Dewey without naming him. The problem is that the phrase became an empty mantra: countries repeat the slogan and maintain authoritarian schools.

* * *

## **6\. Contradictions and limitations**

Dewey was too optimistic about consensus. He believed that rational deliberation would resolve deep conflicts. He underestimated that certain conflicts—fundamental values and opposing material interests—do not yield to dialogue. History showed that powerful groups frequently block participation not out of ignorance but out of interest: popular participation threatens privilege.

He also ran into the problem of scale without solving it. The Laboratory School had a few dozen children. Face-to-face communities work. But how do you make millions participate in complex decisions without it becoming chaos or paralyzing assembly-ism? Dewey died in 1952 without finding the technical answer. The answer would come after him, from another place: distributed collaboration tools.

* * *

## **7\. The translation into versioning**

Dewey never saw a computer. He died three years before the first commercial transistor reached the market. Acknowledging the anachronism is mandatory. But his ideas describe with precision what a good versioning system does.

An objection comes up here frequently: “Dewey was describing the education of children in an experimental school, not the governance of adults with conflicting interests, unequal power, and legal obligations. The analogy doesn’t transfer.” The objection is right about the context. It is wrong about the core problem. What Dewey identified — that participation is a skill that is learned or not learned according to the environment — is identical in adults. A cooperative whose members were never invited to propose anything, question anything, or review anything produces exactly what the authoritarian school produced: people who know they have a right to a voice and don’t know how to use it. Versioning solves the part Dewey had no way to solve: the technical problem of scale. How to make many people participate in an organized way. The pedagogical part remains the responsibility of whoever implements the system.

This article has no single anchor step in the grid. Its role is transversal: Dewey justifies the entire process of the 21 Steps as democratic pedagogy in action. Issue (Step 9) is the investigation that begins when someone names the problem. Branch (Step 10) is the hypothesis that is tested without compromising the main line. Review (Step 12) is evaluation by peers, not by hierarchy. Merge or Revert (Steps 13 and 15) are the results that teach: incorporated or discarded, the participant learns from the real consequence. Fork (Step 18) is the right to disagree without disappearing. Each complete cycle is, in Dewey’s sense, a lesson in democracy practiced.

One point Dewey left incomplete that versioning partially resolves: good faith. Dewey trusted that rational participation would lead to consensus. He did not anticipate bad-faith actors who use the process to consolidate power. The versioning system does not solve this, but it makes the deviation visible. Who captures the merge, who systematically blocks issues, and who approves without reviewing: all of it is recorded in the log. Bad faith is not impossible. It is, however, conducted in public. The political cost of acting against the process in a transparent system is higher than acting that way in an opaque one. That difference is not small.

The warning: Dewey knew that formal participation without real conditions is theater. If the repository is “open,” but nobody teaches how to contribute; if the language is too technical; if those who ask questions are ignored—the openness is a facade. Governance as versioning requires investing in the capacity of participants, not just in the architecture of the system.

## **8\. Practical implication**

In 1950, Anísio Teixeira inaugurated the Escola Parque in Salvador, Bahia. The project was ambitious even by current standards: children spent the school day in full-time sessions, alternating between traditional academic activities and laboratories of arts, theater, music, physical education, and work workshops. What distinguished the Escola Parque was not the curriculum—it was the methodology: students participated in planning activities, evaluated their own learning alongside teachers, and held real responsibilities over shared spaces.

The result that evaluators of the time documented was not only academic. It was behavioral: children who went through the Escola Parque arrived in secondary school with skills of argumentation, negotiation, and conflict resolution that their peers from conventional schools did not have. Dewey would have recognized it immediately: they had not learned the theory of democracy. They had practiced democracy every day for years. The skill was in the muscle, not in the memory.

A cooperative that adopts versioned governance needs something beyond a repository and rules: it needs pedagogy. Teaching members to open issues. Teaching constructive reviews. Teaching that making mistakes is part of the process. If the system is implemented without this educational layer, it becomes a tool for the few. Those who already know how to participate do. Those who don’t, watch. Dewey would call that a cathedral disguised as a bazaar.

* * *

## **9\. Lessons learned**

Democracy is a skill, not a talent. It is learned by doing, not by watching.  
A school that trains obedience produces passive citizens. That is not a coincidence: it is design.  
The public has not disappeared. It is invisible to itself. Transparency tools help rebuild it.  
Participation without education is a facade. Openness without capacity-building reproduces the hierarchy it promised to eliminate.  
Nobody is born knowing how to govern. But anyone can learn if the system is designed to teach.

## **10\. Exploring paths and connections**

Dewey closes Part II with the missing piece: the human engine. Systems need feedback (Meadows), but feedback only works if someone participates in the loop. And participation, Dewey showed, is a learned skill, not innate talent.

Part III begins with a different question: given all of this, how do you coordinate many people without destroying the openness?

We are in 2004. An American journalist named James Surowiecki publishes a book with a title that sounds like a joke: _The Wisdom of Crowds_. The thesis is counterintuitive: under certain conditions (diversity, independence, decentralization, and an aggregation mechanism), large groups are more accurate than individual experts. Not because people are brilliant. But because, when there are many of them and they think differently, errors cancel out.

If Dewey showed that participation is learned by doing, Surowiecki will show that crowds that participate can be smarter than any single expert. The question shifts from “How do you teach participation?” to “How do you aggregate the participation of millions?”

We mentioned in two articles (Mises and Meadows) the protagonist of Isaac Asimov’s Foundation books, called Hari Seldon or simply Seldon.  
Dewey and Seldon make the same bet by opposite paths: Dewey believes that democracy is learned by doing in the present. Seldon believes that civilization is saved by anticipating the future. Both would recognize in the other the same instinct: systems do not survive by accident—they survive by deliberate design of learning.

Dewey wanted to teach citizens to govern the present. Seldon, in fiction, wanted to teach civilizations to survive the future. Both arrived at the same place: neither the present nor the future governs itself. Both require protocol, records, and space to make mistakes with reversible consequences.
