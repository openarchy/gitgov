### **The lawyer who explained the impossible**

#### **Article 16 of 21—Governance as Versioning series**

* * *

 _This is Article 16. The Governance as Versioning series maps 335 years of political, philosophical, and systemic thought to the logic of code versioning. Article 16 covers Yochai Benkler, the transversal concept of the repository as a commons managed by peers—the foundation that gives meaning to all 21 steps of the protocol—and the question that connects Surowiecki to Lessig: who writes the rules that make collective production possible, and who may be hiding inside them?_

* * *

**What you’ll find here:**

This article explains how people with no boss, no salary, and no contract manage to produce things that corporations cannot. And it shows how that way of working is, in fact, the most precise description of what a collective versioning system does: it transforms a shared space into a productive resource, as long as the rules are clear and access is open.

* * *

### **1\. Where it all begins**

New Haven, Connecticut, spring of 2002. A 37-year-old Israeli law professor walks across the Yale campus carrying a 78-page manuscript that no economics journal wanted to publish. The title mixed Nobel laureate Ronald Coase, a software mascot penguin, and the question no economist had managed to answer properly.

The question was simple. How do thousands of people scattered across the planet—with no boss, no employment contract, no salary—manage to produce software that rivals the products of billion-dollar corporations?

Yochai Benkler was not a programmer. He was a lawyer. But he had done something most economists did not bother to do: he looked at Linux, at Wikipedia, and at Apache (the program running most of the world’s websites at the time) and realized there was no market or state at work. There was a third thing. Something that traditional economics said should not exist.

In 2006, he published _The Wealth of Networks_ , 500 pages that gave a name to what was happening right under everyone’s nose. He called it commons-based peer production: people contribute of their own free will, without formal hierarchy, and the result belongs to everyone. Not charity. Not a hobby. A mode of production that, for certain types of goods—information, knowledge, software, culture—works better than a company and better than a government.

Economists hated it. Four years later, Elinor Ostrom (Article 12 in this series) won the Nobel Prize by proving that commons work. Benkler had been right. He just arrived too early.

* * *

### **2\. Historical context**

In 2006, the world still operated with two paths: either the market solves it (company pays, employee works, shareholder profits) or the state solves it (government plans, civil servant executes, taxpayer pays). A third way was a seminar joke.

But the facts did not cooperate. The Encyclopædia Britannica, with two hundred years of tradition, was losing to an encyclopedia written by anonymous volunteers. A 2005 Nature study concluded that Wikipedia had a comparable number of errors to the Britannica. Linux, built by people receiving no salary for it, ran more than 90% of internet servers. Apache dominated the web without a single shareholder or CEO.

Orthodox economics had no vocabulary to explain what was happening. Benkler created that vocabulary.

* * *

### **3\. The central idea**

Benkler’s thesis fits in a paragraph. When the cost of coordinating people falls—and the internet did exactly that—a mode of production emerges that needs neither a company nor a government. People collaborate for varied reasons: curiosity, reputation, the desire to solve their own problems, and belief in a cause. The result belongs to everyone. Benkler called this commons-based peer production.

Think of a street where neighbors tend a community garden. No one owns it. No one receives a salary. Each person plants what they know, harvests what they need, and tends when they can. If someone drops out, another steps in. If someone plants wrong, the others correct it. It works not because people are saints, but because the cost of participating is low and the benefit is shared.

Before Benkler, this kind of collaboration was thought to work only at a small scale: fishing villages and rural communities (Ostrom documented this). Benkler showed that the internet scaled it to millions. Wikipedia has 60 million articles in 300 languages. Linux runs the planet’s infrastructure. These are not exceptions. They are the model operating at an industrial scale without having anything industrial about it.

* * *

### **4\. Developments**

**The myth of salary as the only engine**

Ronald Coase, Nobel laureate in economics, asked in 1937, “Why do companies exist?” Answer: because coordinating people through market contracts is too expensive. It is cheaper to bring everyone under one roof, pay a salary, and give orders. The company exists because hierarchy is cheaper than constant negotiation.

Benkler turned Coase upside down. When the internet drove the cost of coordination close to zero, the company’s advantage shrank. It did not disappear (factories still need hierarchy), but for everything involving information, knowledge, and culture, coordinating among peers became cheaper than maintaining offices, managers, and HR departments. That is why Wikipedia needs no company. The cost of organizing volunteer editors through a wiki is lower than the cost of hiring a professional newsroom.

**Modularity: the secret of scale**

Benkler identified the condition that allows thousands of strangers to collaborate: modularity. Large tasks divided into small pieces. Each person contributes to the piece they know, in the time they have. Five minutes correcting a spelling error on Wikipedia is a valid contribution. Half an hour writing a paragraph about neighborhood history is a valid contribution. An entire weekend refactoring a Linux module is a valid contribution.

The point is that no one needs to understand the whole system. Each person tends their piece, and the review system weaves the pieces into a coherent whole. Like a community barn-raising where each neighbor does what they know: one lays bricks, another runs the wiring, and another paints. The result is a house none of them could have built alone.

**Diverse motivation as strength, not weakness**

Economics assumes everyone works for the same reason: money. Benkler showed that diversity of motivation is an advantage, not a problem. In Linux, there are people who contribute for intellectual pleasure, people who contribute to learn, people paid by companies that use the system and need it to work, and people who contribute out of ideology. This mix makes the project more resilient. If one motivation weakens (the company cuts the budget), the others sustain it.

Compare that to a company where everyone depends on salary. If the money runs out, everyone leaves. In a peer project, if one source dries up, the others compensate. Antifragility by design (Taleb, Article 20, will go deeper into this).

* * *

### **5\. Historical legacy**

The concept of peer production spread beyond software. Creative Commons (open licenses for texts, music, and images) exists because of this logic. OpenStreetMap (the volunteer-built map that Google Maps uses in several countries) is peer production. Open science, with repositories like arXiv and shared research data, accelerates discoveries by eliminating the paywall barrier. The rapid sequencing of the SARS-CoV-2 genome in 2020 depended on researchers publishing data in real time, without waiting for paid journal review.

In Brazil, the Open Data Portal and projects like Querido Diário (which turns official municipal gazettes into searchable data) apply the same logic: public information belongs to everyone, and organized volunteers produce tools the state would not produce on its own.

* * *

### **6\. Contradictions and limitations**

Benkler had blind spots. The main one: unequal access. Peer production requires internet access, free time, and, in most cases, command of English. Open software communities are, to this day, predominantly composed of white men from wealthy countries. If initial access is unequal, the meritocracy of code reproduces the inequalities it promised to overcome.

Second point: invisible labor. Maintaining old code, moderating conflicts, and writing documentation—the unglamorous work that sustains the commons rarely receives the same recognition as a brilliant contribution. The 2014 OpenSSL collapse (Heartbleed, a vulnerability that affected half the internet) revealed that a library used by billions was being maintained by a single person with no resources. Volunteerism at scale has costs that someone pays, even when the bill does not show up.

### **7\. The translation into versioning**

**A legitimate objection before continuing:** “If peer production works so well, why do so many open projects die from abandonment, capture, or internal conflict?” The answer lies precisely in what Benkler identified but many ignored: commons without explicit rules is not freedom—it is no-man’s-land. Versioning does not resolve this by magic, but it offers a structure where the rules are written down, visible, and enforceable by any participant, not just by the founders.

Benkler never used the phrase “governance as versioning.” He described the mechanics of peer production. The protocol provides the map.

The transversal concept Benkler illuminates in this series is the **repository as a commons managed by peers** : a space of collective ownership, with clear rules of access, contribution, and review, where no version is privately owned and every change passes through distributed assessment. It is not a specific step in the protocol: it is the foundation that gives meaning to all the others. Every commit (a recorded change), every review (an assessment), and every merge (the approval of new content) only makes sense because there is a commons worth protecting.

**First translation: the repository as commons.** In Git, the code belongs to the group. No one owns the main branch. Anyone can propose a change (a pull request—a formal request for a contribution to enter the official document); anyone can review it. The license (GPL, MIT, or Apache) is the social contract that protects the commons from capture. In human governance, the README (the “house manual,” Step 8 of the 21 steps) does the same: it defines the rules that protect the shared space.

**Second translation: review as distributed quality filtering.** Benkler showed that Wikipedia needs no editor-in-chief because thousands of eyes catch errors. In versioning, peer review is exactly that. A proposal does not enter the official version because an important person approved it. It enters because other peers verified it. Quality emerges from the process, not from authority.

**Third translation: modularity as participation design.** Issues (written proposals, Step 9) are modules. Each issue is a small piece so that someone with half an hour free can contribute. If the system demands total dedication, it excludes 95% of people. Benkler proved that granularity is what allows thousands to participate without anyone needing to become a professional in governance.

**Vigilance:** if the commons is not protected by clear rules, someone will capture it. Amazon uses open software and profits billions without contributing back in the same proportion. In a neighborhood association, the member who dominates the process and monopolizes the reviews turns the bazaar into a personal cathedral. The 21 steps include Fork (Step 19) as a release valve: if someone captures the project, the group can leave and start over, taking the entire history with them. A fork is not failure—it is the right of exit that prevents founder tyranny.

**A limitation of versioning does not resolve on its own:** Benkler’s model assumes that participants enter in good faith, even with varied motivations. The versioning protocol does not prevent sabotage, deliberately bad contributions, or malicious occupation of review roles. What the system does is make every action traceable and every decision auditable: whoever acted in bad faith leaves a permanent trace. It is not a guarantee of honesty—it is the political cost of dishonesty.

### **8\. Practical implication**

**Querido Diário: peer production applied to public transparency in Brazil**

Querido Diário is an Open Knowledge Brasil project that transforms the official municipal gazettes of Brazilian cities (the documents where governments publish acts, contracts, and appointments, usually as unreadable PDFs) into searchable and reusable data. Launched in 2021, it operates exactly on Benkler’s model: no formal hierarchy, with dozens of volunteers contributing small modules (parsers—small programs that “read” each different PDF format), no salary, and results that belong to everyone under an open license.

Each municipality has its own official gazette format. Each volunteer adopts one or two municipalities, writes the reading module for that specific format, and submits it for review. Other volunteers check and suggest adjustments. No one needs to understand the whole project: each person tends their piece. By 2024, the project covered hundreds of municipalities and was used by journalists, researchers, and transparency advocates.

Modularity enabled what no government procurement process could achieve: diversity of coverage without centralized bureaucracy. And Benkler’s limitation appeared too: smaller municipalities, with fewer interested volunteers, fell behind. The commons formed where motivation existed, not necessarily where need was greatest.

One more example. A recycling cooperative needs to decide whether to accept a new contract with the local government. Traditional model: the president negotiates, presents a finished proposal at the assembly, and asks for a vote. Peer model: the proposal is published as an issue. Three cooperative members with contract experience review the terms. Two members who work in sorting weigh in on operational feasibility. Each contributes to the piece they know. The final decision is better informed, the process is faster, and no one needs to understand the whole contract to participate in what they know.

### **9\. Lessons learned**

There are not only two paths (market or state). There is a third: people collaborating with one another, with no boss, with clear rules.

Motivation is not only money. Curiosity, reputation, the desire to solve a problem, belief in a cause—all of these move people. And the mix is more resilient than any single incentive.

Modularity is the key to scale. If you require someone to dedicate their whole life to participate, you will have five participants. If you accept fifteen-minute contributions, you will have thousands.

Commons without rules become no-man’s-land. With clear rules, they become the most productive resource there is.

A fork is not a fracture: it is the right of exit that prevents capture. A commons that does not allow exit is a trap.

* * *

### **10\. Exploring paths and connections**

Benkler took the next step after Surowiecki: he explained how crowds organize themselves in practice. It is not magic. It is peer production: small modules, diverse motivation, distributed review, and results that belong to everyone.

But one question hangs in the air. Everything Benkler described depends on how the system is built. The rules of the game, the code running underneath, and the permissions that define who can do what. What if someone writes those rules in a way that looks open but is not?

We are in 2006, the same year as Benkler’s book. At Stanford, a law professor named Lawrence Lessig publishes the second version of a cutting argument: code is law. Not a metaphor. A fact. Whoever writes the system’s code defines what is possible, what is forbidden, and what is easy or difficult. If the repository’s code does not allow a fork, dissent does not exist. If it does, it is a right guaranteed by design.

Benkler showed that peers can produce without a boss. Lessig will show that the boss may be hiding inside the code.
