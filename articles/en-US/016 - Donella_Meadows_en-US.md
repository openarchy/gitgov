### **The thermostat no one sees**

#### **Article 13 of 21 of the Governance as Versioning series**

 _This is Article 13. The thinker is Donella Meadows. The central Git concept is FEEDBACK — the loop that makes the system see itself. In the 21 Steps, this article does not anchor a single step. It cuts across all of them: Meadows justifies the entire cycle as a machine for short feedback loops._

* * *

#### **What will you find here?**

Have you ever seen an organization repeat the same mistake for years, as if each new leadership started from zero, unaware of what the previous one tried? Have you ever been part of a group that was fixing the wrong problem while the real cause grew quietly in the background?

Meadows spent her entire life understanding why systems do this. And she found an answer that fits in a word everyone knows but almost no one uses correctly: feedback.

* * *

## **1\. Where it all begins**

Hanover, New Hampshire, winter of 1972. A 30-year-old biophysicist sits in front of a wardrobe-sized computer in the basement of MIT and runs a simulation that will irritate the entire planet. Her name is Donella Meadows. The program is called World3. The result, published as “The Limits to Growth” for the Club of Rome, says the following: if economic growth continues unchecked, the planet’s resources will collapse before the end of the 21st century.

Economists ridiculed it. Politicians ignored it. Journalists called it catastrophism. Meadows did not argue back. She returned to the laboratory. Fifty years later, the climate crisis and the collapse of supply chains confirmed the curves of that old computer.

But Meadows did not stay trapped in the prediction. She spent three decades trying to understand why systems do what they do. Why do some self-correct and others sink? Why does the thermostat in your home work while the “thermostat” of the global economy seems broken?

The answer, condensed in the book “Thinking in Systems” (published in 2008, seven years after her death), fits in a word everyone has heard, but almost no one truly understands: feedback. The result of an action that comes back and influences the next action. It sounds simple. It changes everything.

* * *

## **2\. Historical context**

Meadows’s world was that of the Cold War and the race for growth. Capitalists and socialists disagreed on almost everything but shared one premise: more is better. More production, more consumption, more extraction. The idea that growth could have physical limits was heresy on both sides of the iron curtain.

The dominant thinking was linear: a problem appears, a solution is applied, the problem disappears. Meadows challenged this. Most problems do not disappear with point solutions because they are circular: today’s solution becomes tomorrow’s problem, and tomorrow’s problem feeds yesterday’s. Systems do not operate in a straight line. They operate in loops.

* * *

## **3\. The central idea**

Feedback is when the result of an action comes back and influences the next action. You put your hand on the hot stove, feel pain, and withdraw your hand. The pain is feedback. Without it, you would burn down to the bone.

Meadows identified two types of feedback that govern any system.

Negative feedback stabilizes. It is the thermostat in your home. The temperature rises above 22 degrees, the air conditioning turns on, and the temperature falls. It falls below 22, the heater turns on, and the temperature rises. The system stays near the desired point. “Negative” here is not bad: it is the car’s brake, the alarm that rings, and the correction that prevents disaster.

Positive feedback amplifies. It is a microphone pressed against a speaker. Sound enters, amplifies, and exits through the speaker, entering again and amplifying further until that unbearable screech. Or a real estate bubble: the price rises, people buy to resell, the price rises more, and more people buy until it bursts. “Positive” is not good: it is an accelerator with no brake.

Meadows’s great insight: healthy systems have dominant negative feedback. They self-correct. Sick systems have uncontrolled positive feedback. They amplify the error until they collapse. The difference between a stable economy and a financial crisis is not in the people: it is in the loops. Whether the brake works or whether someone cut the cable.

* * *

## **4\. Developments**

### **The delay that kills**

Feedback needs to be fast to work. If you touch the stove and the pain arrives ten minutes later, you will burn your entire hand before withdrawing it. Meadows showed that most serious systemic problems come from delayed feedback: the information about the error exists but takes too long to reach whoever can correct it.

A politician approves a bad law. The effects appear in two or three years. The election is four years away. By the time the voter discovers the law was terrible, the politician has already been reelected or moved to another office. The feedback existed but arrived too late. The system did not self-correct in time.

In fishing, the example is brutal. Fishermen take too many fish. The population falls, but the effect only appears in the next season. By the time the collapse becomes visible, it has already passed the point of recovery.

### **Where the loop disconnects**

Worse than slow feedback is blocked feedback. Meadows drew attention to systems in which information about the result does not reach the decision-maker. It is not delay: it is a wall. A manager who refuses to read outcome reports has zero feedback. A dictatorship that censors the press does not operate with delayed information: it operates without information. The loop was cut.

Meadows was direct: a system without feedback is a blind system. It may function by inertia. But when the problem appears, it appears all at once. Without warning. Like a driver who blindfolded himself: he does not crash immediately, but he will crash.

### **The twelve places to push**

Meadows proposed a hierarchy of twelve leverage points: the places to intervene in a system to produce the greatest effect. At the base, weak interventions include adjusting numbers, tweaking parameters, and increasing a budget by 10%. At the top, strong interventions: changing the flow of information, rewriting the rules, changing the system’s goal, and changing the paradigm.

The paradox she found: people intervene where it is easy (adjusting numbers) and ignore where it works (changing rules and information). Politicians love to cut ribbons and announce funding. No one campaigns on the promise, “I will change the information flow in the health system.” But it is the second intervention that actually works.

## **5\. Historical legacy**

“The Limits to Growth” sold more than 30 million copies and was translated into 37 languages. Meadows’s models directly influenced the creation of the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) in 1988. The concept of “leverage points” became an analytical tool in public policy, business management, and urban planning.

In 2008, the European Environment Agency reviewed the original models and concluded that the 1972 curves were within the predicted margin. Meadows herself, in a 2004 update, redid the numbers. They were worse than the originals, not better. She died in 2001 without seeing the publication of “Thinking in Systems,” edited by Diana Wright. The book became a reference in systems thinking for non-specialists.

* * *

## **6\. Contradictions and limitations**

Meadows thought in systems with the clarity of someone who spent a lifetime modeling on a computer. That was both her strength and her weakness. Her models worked well for physical resources (oil, water, and fish) but lost precision with social systems, in which variables change meaning according to context. Human behavior does not obey differential equations with the same docility as carbon stocks.

The hierarchy of twelve leverage points, despite its elegance, suffers from a circular problem: to know which point to attack, you already need to understand the system. And if you already understand it, you probably do not need the hierarchy. It is a diagnostic tool for those who have already done half the diagnosis.

## **7\. The translation into versioning**

Meadows never saw a Git repository. She had never heard of commit, merge, or revert. But the vocabulary she created to describe systems is the vocabulary of versioning translated into engineering.

An objection appears here frequently: “Systems thinking is a tool for engineering and large organizations. A neighborhood cooperative, a parents’ association, or a community council does not need to model feedback loops.” The objection underestimates what Meadows discovered. Every group that makes repeated decisions over time already operates with loops, whether it knows it or not. The question is not whether the loops exist: it is whether they are fast, visible, and controllable. An association that approves a rule, waits two years to see the effect, and has no way to reverse it without calling a new assembly has slow feedback and a partially blocked loop. Meadows is not asking for computational modeling. She is asking you to ask, “How long does it take for an error to be detected and corrected here?”

This article, like those on Johnson and Kahneman, has no single anchor step. Its role is transversal: Meadows justifies the complete cycle of the 21 Steps as a machine for short loops. Issue (Step 9) is the sensor that detects the temperature; without it, the system goes blind at that point. Review (Step 12) is feedback before action, the equivalent of testing the medicine before applying it to the patient. Merge (Step 13) is controlled positive feedback: this works; incorporate it. Revert (Step 15) is negative feedback: this went wrong; undo it. And the short deadline of the entire cycle is the antidote to the delay that kills. In a representative democracy, the loop takes four years. In a well-managed repository, it takes hours or days.

The vigilance alert is direct. If the repository accepts a merge without review, the feedback loop is cut. If issues go weeks without response, the delay is already killing it. If no one reads the commit log, the sensor exists, but no one is looking at the panel. Meadows would say, “You built the thermostat but disconnected the wire.”

In Article 04 of the series, on Ludwig Von Mises, we talked about Isaac Asimov and his Foundation series. In it, Seldon’s Plan is a long-term feedback system: each generation receives signals about what is working, adjusts, and passes it on to the next. The “Vault” in the series is literally a deferred feedback mechanism—a message from the past to the future saying, “Here is what the system predicted; compare it with what happened.” In one sentence:

Asimov called Seldon’s Plan what Meadows would call an intergenerational feedback loop: a system that learns over centuries because someone took care to record the original premises alongside the expected result. Without that record, each generation starts from zero.

## **8\. Practical implication**

In 2001, Estonia launched X-Road, a digital infrastructure system that connected all the country’s public services in a single data-exchange network. Income tax, medical prescriptions, property records, and driver’s licenses: each database talked to the others in real time, with a complete log of who accessed what and when. A citizen filled in a tax declaration in five minutes because the data was already there. A doctor saw a complete history because the records communicated. Any data error could be traced back to its source within hours.

Meadows would have recognized it immediately: X-Road was designed as a fast feedback system. Information about any point in the system was available to whoever needed to make the next decision. No weeks-long delay waiting for a paper report. No loop blocked by a bureaucracy that does not talk to another bureaucracy. The result was that Estonia began correcting inconsistencies in public data in real time, rather than discovering them in an annual audit. The sensor was connected. The wire had not been cut.

Now imagine a cooperative that manages a neighborhood farmers’ market. The vendors complain that the stall allocation rules are unfair. Without feedback, the board only discovers the discontent when half the vendors have left. With fast feedback—an open complaints channel, a monthly meeting with a public record, and the ability to reverse a rule that did not work—the correction happens in weeks. The loop is short. No one needs to wait for the next board election to fix what is wrong right now.

## **9\. Lessons learned**

Every system depends on feedback. If information about the result does not reach the decision-maker, the system is blind. No matter how many resources it has.

Slow feedback is almost as dangerous as zero feedback. A correction that arrives after the damage is not a correction: it is an autopsy.

Adjusting numbers is the easiest intervention and the least effective. Changing rules, information, and goals is difficult, but it is what works.

A good system is not one that has no problems. It is one that detects and corrects quickly. The thermostat does not prevent the temperature from rising. It prevents it from rising without anyone noticing.

* * *

## **10\. Exploring paths and connections**

Meadows reveals the engine that Part II had been describing all along without naming it: feedback loops. If the loop works, the system self-corrects. If it does not, it degrades. It is the difference between an organization that learns and one that repeats the same mistakes until it collapses.

But a thermostat alone solves nothing. Someone needs to set the desired temperature. Someone needs to turn on the device. Someone needs to be in the room. Feedback only works if there are people participating in the loop. And participating in a collective system is not a talent you are born with: it is a skill you learn by doing.

In 1916, an American philosopher named John Dewey published “Democracy and Education” with a thesis that unsettled many people: democracy is not a system of government; it is a way of life. Where Lippmann (article 9) saw incapable citizens who needed elites, Dewey saw people who never had the chance to learn how to decide. The problem is not that people are unintelligent. It is that no one ever taught anyone how to participate.

Meadows’s loops need people inside them. Dewey will show how to put people in.
