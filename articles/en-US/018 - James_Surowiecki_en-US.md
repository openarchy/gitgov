### **The Wisdom of Crowds**

#### **Article 15 of 21 — Governance as Versioning series**

 _This is Article 15. The thinker is James Surowiecki, journalist and author of_ The Wisdom of Crowds _(2004). The central Git concept is Merge Conflict—the moment when two independent drafts collide and the system demands an explicit decision. This corresponds to Step 20 of the 21-Step protocol: the penultimate step before any version is considered final._

* * *

#### **What you will find here**

This article shows why a group of ordinary people, under the right conditions, decides better than any isolated expert — and how that discovery connects directly to the heart of collective versioning: the moment when two diverging drafts need to be reconciled.

* * *

### **1\. Where it all begins**

Plymouth, England, autumn of 1906. The Annual West of England Fat Stock and Poultry Exhibition drew farmers, butchers, housewives, and the curious. The smell of manure mixed with fried fish and chips. At the center of the event, a fattened ox stood motionless under the gaze of nearly 800 people who had each paid sixpence to bet on how much the animal would weigh after slaughter and preparation.

Francis Galton was there. He was 85, fleeing the London winter on doctors’ orders. A statistician, Darwin’s cousin, and a man who had spent his life trying to prove that intelligence was concentrated in an elite. He bought the used tickets after the contest. He wanted to demonstrate the ignorance of the masses.

He sat down at a table. He sorted 787 legible tickets. He calculated the median of the estimates.

The crowd’s median: 1,207 pounds. The ox’s actual weight: 1,198 pounds. A margin of error of 0.8%.

Galton published the result in the journal Nature in March 1907, under the title “Vox Populi.” He called the finding “more favorable to the reliability of democratic judgment than might have been expected.” For a man who had spent his career defending eugenics, the sentence was almost a confession of defeat.

A hundred years later, a New Yorker journalist named James Surowiecki opened a book with that story. He was 37 and had dropped out of a history doctorate at Yale to write about finance. He spent years collecting similar examples: prediction markets that outperformed experts and game show audiences that were more accurate than the guest contestants. The pattern was consistent. But it had one enormous asterisk.

* * *

### **2\. Historical context**

In 2004, the world was nursing a double hangover. The dot-com bubble had burst four years earlier. September 11 had shaken confidence in every form of institutional prediction. The consensus swung between two extremes: trust experts (who had failed) or trust no one (which was paralyzing).

Surowiecki entered that vacuum with a third option. The answer lay in the conditions under which a group operates. Crowds could be brilliant or catastrophic. The difference wasn’t who participated, but how.

* * *

### **3\. The central idea**

Surowiecki’s thesis fits in one sentence: under the right conditions, a large group of ordinary people gets it more right than any single expert.

The right conditions are four. Diversity of opinion: people need to think differently, not repeat the same reasoning. Independence of judgment: each person decides on their own, without copying their neighbor. Decentralization: people use local information that only they have. An aggregation mechanism: there is a way to combine all the guesses into one result.

Go back to the Plymouth ox. The 787 bettors came from different worlds. The farmer estimated from experience with breeds. The butcher compared visible fat with scale weight. The housewife guessed by size. Each was wrong in a different way. Some too high, some too low. When errors are diverse and independent, they cancel each other out. What remains is surprisingly close to the truth.

Before Surowiecki, conventional wisdom said crowds were dumb and dangerous. Gustave Le Bon published _The Crowd_ in 1895 and convinced generations that the group always degrades the individual. After Surowiecki, the question changed. It’s not “are crowds wise or stupid.” It’s: What conditions make the difference?

The answer: violate any one of the four conditions, and the wisdom disappears.

* * *

### **4\. Developments**

**When the herd eats the map**

The most fragile condition is independence. All it takes is for people to start copying each other, and the crowd turns into a herd. Solomon Asch demonstrated this in a laboratory in the 1950s: he placed a person in a room with seven actors who gave wrong answers about the length of lines. Even when they could see the correct answer, 75% of participants agreed with the group at least once.

Financial bubbles are herds in slow motion. An investor sees prices rising, buys because others are buying. He doesn’t evaluate the asset: he imitates behavior. The dot-com bubble didn’t burst from lack of information. It burst because the information circulating was imitation disguised as analysis. When independence dies, the crowd becomes an error amplifier.

**The filter nobody designed**

The most counterintuitive condition is diversity. Not demographic diversity (though that helps), but cognitive diversity: different ways of thinking about the same problem. The farmer and the butcher don’t think about the ox in the same way. That’s what makes the average work.

Surowiecki showed that homogeneous groups make more mistakes than heterogeneous groups, even when the homogeneous ones consist of experts. A committee of five economists may be less accurate than a group of twenty people with varied professions. Not because economists are bad, but because their errors tend in the same direction. Similar biases add up instead of canceling out. Kahneman (article 10) had already shown the shortcuts of the brain. Surowiecki showed that the cure for those shortcuts isn’t smarter people: it’s different people.

**The invisible mortar: how to combine guesses**

None of the first three conditions is worth anything without the fourth: an aggregation mechanism. The Plymouth crowd, without Galton calculating the median, would have been mere noise. The wisdom was in the tickets, but it needed someone — or something — to bring it all together.

Good aggregation transforms scattered opinions into a decision. Bad aggregation amplifies distortions. Binary voting (yes or no) is poor aggregation: 51% wins, 49% disappears. Averaging estimates is rich aggregation: it incorporates the full distribution. Surowiecki didn’t say every form of voting works. He said that the way opinions are combined determines whether the crowd will be wise or stupid.

### **5\. Historical legacy**

The book sold hundreds of thousands of copies and changed vocabularies. “Wisdom of crowds” became a common expression in technology, business, and politics. Wikipedia, which was two years old at the time, became the living example of the thesis: thousands of anonymous editors producing an encyclopedia more accurate than Britannica in several categories, according to a 2005 Nature study. Google, Microsoft, and HP created internal prediction markets to estimate project timelines and product success, with results better than those of their project managers.

The “Who Wants to Be a Millionaire” program illustrated the thesis every week: the “ask the audience” option was correct 91% of the time, compared to 65% for the “phone a friend” option (the expert).

* * *

### **6\. Contradictions and limitations**

Surowiecki had a blind spot. His theory works best for problems with verifiable answers (weight of an ox, election results, project deadlines) and less well for questions of value (what is just, how to distribute resources, what is the best social policy). When there is no “right answer” against which to measure, the wisdom of crowds becomes harder to demonstrate and easier to confuse with the tyranny of the majority. Surowiecki himself acknowledged the distinction, but many readers missed the nuance.

Furthermore, the internet, which should have amplified collective wisdom, frequently destroys the condition of independence. Social media algorithms create bubbles where everyone sees the same content, imitates the same reactions, and reinforces the same biases. The wisdom of crowds requires independence. Personalized feeds produce the opposite.

### **7\. The translation into versioning**

#### **Legitimate objection before continuing:**

“If the crowd is so wise, why does Git have a command called Merge Conflict — which is precisely the moment when two drafts diverge and clash?” The answer is because divergence is not a failure. It’s the signal that independence worked. Merge Conflict (Step 20 of the protocol, the penultimate step before any version is considered final) is the moment when the system forces the group to look directly at the differences and decide what stays, what goes, and why. Without that explicit conflict, the apparent harmony would be nothing but imitation disguised as consensus.

Surowiecki never used Git. He wrote on a metaphorical typewriter (he was a magazine journalist). But the four conditions he identified describe with surgical precision why open versioning works when it works, and why it fails when it fails.

**Diversity:** in an open repository (a shared space where multiple people store and modify documents with a complete history), contributors come from different countries, backgrounds, and contexts. The developer in Bangalore sees bugs the developer in Berlin doesn’t see. Not because one is better, but because they look from different angles. Five reviewers with distinct experiences are more reliable than one brilliant reviewer. Surowiecki explained why: diverse errors cancel out; homogeneous errors accumulate.

**Independence:** in Git, each contributor works on their own branch (a separate draft, like a personal copy of the document that hasn’t yet been sent to the group) before submitting. They don’t see each other’s drafts until the review stage. That separation isn’t a design accident: it’s protection against imitation. The moment everyone starts copying the most popular contributor’s code, the repository loses the crowd’s advantage. It becomes a digital herd.

**Decentralization:** Hayek (article 3) showed that knowledge is dispersed. Surowiecki added, “Dispersal rises and only works if the system allows local knowledge to enter.” Each issue (a problem notification or suggestion, like a note stuck on the group’s bulletin board) opened by someone who uses the system day to day carries information no central committee possesses. An association where only the board can propose agenda items violates decentralization. If any member can open an issue, the system aggregates local knowledge.

**Aggregation and the role of Merge Conflict:** peer review is an aggregation mechanism. A merge approved by multiple reviewers is the governance equivalent of the calculation Galton made with the Plymouth tickets. Merge Conflict (Step 20) is the stress test of that mechanism: when two independent drafts change the same part of a document in incompatible ways, the system stops and demands an explicit decision. No one can go around it. It is the documentary proof that real diversity existed and that convergence was negotiated, not forced.

Surowiecki’s warning applies in full: if the review is dominated by one person (the “maintainer” who approves everything alone), aggregation is poor. If the merge depends on forced consensus (everyone must agree), aggregation becomes rigid. The system needs diverse, independent reviewers and a clear process for turning reviews into decisions.

**Vigilance:** the most insidious threat in a repository is the loss of independence disguised as consensus. When everyone agrees too quickly, it’s not wisdom: it may be imitation. Surowiecki would teach us to distrust unanimity. If five reviewers approved in two minutes, no one really read it.

**A limitation that versioning doesn’t resolve on its own:** Surowiecki’s four conditions presuppose that participants operate in good faith, even if with different perspectives. The versioning protocol doesn’t prevent anyone from opening false issues, approving drafts without reading them, or voting to deliberately block. What the system does is make every action visible, traceable, and politically costly: whoever acts in bad faith leaves a permanent trace in the history. Transparency doesn’t guarantee honesty, but it makes dishonesty expensive.

### **8\. Practical implication**

**Kalshi, 2024: when the market became a collective oracle**

In 2024, the American platform Kalshi — authorized by the CFTC (the U.S. federal futures markets regulator) to operate prediction markets on real events, including federal elections — produced estimates consistently more accurate than most traditional electoral polls in the U.S. presidential elections. The mechanism was simple: people bet real money on which candidate would win each state. Whoever was wrong lost. Whoever was right won. The real-time price of bets reflected the collective probability implicit in the judgment of thousands of participants with real incentives to research and reason carefully.

This is Surowiecki at an industrial scale. Diversity (people with different mental models and information sources), independence (each person bet with their own money and analysis), decentralization (local information from each state entering through the behavior of local bettors), and aggregation (the market price as a weighted average of all estimates). When a major newspaper made an error modeling the electorate, the market corrected it in minutes, because there were bettors with different information who profited from the newspaper’s mistake.

The limitation was also Surowiecki: the market worked well for verifiable events (who wins the election) and worse for questions of value (which policy is more just). And like any market, it was vulnerable to coordinated manipulation by groups with resources to distort prices.

Now an illustrative example: a cooperative of family farmers needs to decide whether to accept a new commercial partner. In the cathedral version, the board decides behind closed doors. In the Surowiecki version, all cooperative members share their opinions independently, the system aggregates the opinions, and the decision reflects the group’s intelligence. Not because each member is an expert in contracts, but because each knows something the others don’t. One knows the partner’s reputation in the neighboring town. Another knows how they paid a previous supplier. Another has doubts about the timeline. Put it all together, and the crowd knows more than any single director.

### **9\. Lessons learned**

A crowd is not wise by nature: it is wise by condition. Break diversity, independence, decentralization, or aggregation, and it becomes a herd.

Different people compensate better than smart people. Five median perspectives cancel errors that five brilliant but identical perspectives amplify.

Imitation is the poison of collective intelligence. If everyone agrees too quickly, be suspicious.

Bad aggregation wastes good wisdom. The way opinions are combined matters as much as the opinions themselves.

Merge Conflict is not a failure: it is proof that independence worked. A system that never conflicts probably never had real diversity.

### **10\. Exploring paths and connections**

Part II ended with seven articles on limits: Weber’s bureaucratic cage, Lippmann’s distorted images, Kahneman’s biases, Wurman’s informational anxiety, Ostrom’s commons, Meadows’s feedback, Dewey’s democratic learning. All showed why coordinating people is hard. Part III begins here, asking: hard, yes, but impossible? Surowiecki has just answered: no. Under the right conditions, the crowd surpasses the expert.

But the wise crowd needs to organize. Surowiecki was a journalist, not an engineer. He described the phenomenon; he didn’t build the structure. Who organizes the production of wise crowds without a contract, without a company, without hierarchy?

We are in 2006. A Yale Law professor named Yochai Benkler publishes _The Wealth of Networks_. Thesis: There exists a mode of production that is neither market nor company. People collaborate freely, without salary, without a boss, and the result belongs to everyone. It’s not charity. It’s economically efficient.

Surowiecki showed that crowds can be intelligent. Benkler will show how to put them to work.
