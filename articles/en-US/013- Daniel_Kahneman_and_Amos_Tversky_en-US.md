### **The brain that lies to itself.**

#### **Article 10 of 21 in the series “Governance as Versioning”.**

_This is Article 10 of 21. The thinker is Daniel Kahneman — and, inseparably, Amos Tversky. The Git concepts are peer review (Step 12), fork (Step 18), and the audit trail (Step 14). This article has no single anchor step: its function is transversal. Kahneman doesn’t map to one moment in the versioning process. He explains why the entire process exists._

* * *

#### **What you’ll find here?**

Have you ever made an important decision in a ten-minute meeting and only realized you were wrong weeks later? Have you ever approved something because the proposal was presented in a way that seemed reasonable — not because you checked the data?

Kahneman and Tversky didn’t discover that we’re stupid. They discovered something worse: we’re predictable in the way we err.

* * *

## **1\. Where it all begins.**

Jerusalem, 1969. A psychology seminar at the Hebrew University. Daniel Kahneman, 35 years old, had invited his colleague Amos Tversky to give a talk on decision-making. Tversky had a reputation as a quick genius—the kind who dismantles any argument in thirty seconds. Kahneman was the opposite: methodical, slow, mistrustful of his own conclusions.

After the talk, they went to lunch. The conversation lasted an hour. And then another. And then years.

What they discovered over that lunch and the decades that followed changed what we know about the human mind. Not because they invented an elegant theory. Because they did something more uncomfortable: they proved, with experiments anyone can repeat, that the human brain errs in predictable ways. Not occasionally, out of distraction or laziness. Always. In ways that repeat across any person, any culture, any level of education.

The partnership earned the Nobel Prize in Economics in 2002 (Kahneman alone; Tversky died of cancer in 1996, at 59, and the Nobel is not awarded posthumously). And it created an entire field: behavioral economics, which studies how people actually decide—not how they should.

* * *

## **2\. Historical context.**

In 1969, psychology and economics lived on separate planets. Economists built models based on the “rational agent”: a creature that calculates probabilities, weighs costs and benefits, and maximizes utility. Psychologists knew no one does that. But neither side talked to the other.

The dominant thinking was clear: judgment errors are noise, exceptions, and accidents. Smart, well-informed people decide well. If someone decides poorly, it’s for lack of information or intelligence.

Kahneman and Tversky challenged all of this. Not with opinion. With experiments. They showed that judgment errors are not noise: they’re a pattern. Not exceptions: they’re the normal functioning of the brain. And more: neither intelligence nor education protects against them.

* * *

## **3\. The central idea.**

Thesis in one sentence: the human brain is not a rational machine that occasionally fails. It’s a shortcut machine that works well enough most of the time but errs in predictable and systematic ways.

Kahneman divided thinking into two systems. System 1: fast, automatic, intuitive. It solves “2 + 2” before you even realize you thought about it. System 2: slow, deliberate, analytical. It kicks in when someone asks “17 times 24.”

The problem: System 1 runs almost everything. System 2 is lazy and tiring. In most situations — including important decisions — the brain takes the shortcut and moves on.

A simple analogy. You go to the supermarket to buy rice. Two brands: one costs $4, the other $6. System 1 looks and decides, “The $4 one.” System 2 never wakes up. But if someone asks, “Which has a better price per ounce?“ then System 2 has to work. Most of the time, you don’t do that math.

Now swap rice for a bill, a political candidate, or a building regulation. The mechanism is the same. System 1 decides. System 2 invents the justification afterward.

Before Kahneman and Tversky, the consensus was: give people enough information and they decide well. After them, the answer is different: even with perfect information, the brain processes it crookedly.

## **4\. Developments.**

### **The bias that sticks before you think.**

Anchoring. Kahneman and Tversky had volunteers spin a wheel with numbers from 0 to 100. Then they asked, “How many African countries are members of the UN?” Those who landed on a high number (65, for example) guessed more countries. Those who landed on a low number (10) guessed fewer.

The wheel number was completely random. It had no relation to the question. And yet, it stuck as a reference point.

In governance, anchoring appears every time someone opens a negotiation with an extreme number. “We’ll cut 50% of the budget.” The discussion revolves around the 50%, and any agreement at 20% feels like a win. But a 20% cut can be disastrous. The original number poisoned the judgment.

### **The movie that fear projects.**

Availability heuristic: we judge the probability of something happening by how easily examples come to mind. Sharks kill about five people per year worldwide. Falling coconuts kill over a hundred. But sharks became movies, news, and nightmares. Coconuts did not. Ask anyone which is more dangerous. Most people get it wrong.

This explains why a single case of fraud in a cooperative can paralyze any proposal for transparency (”It’ll go wrong, remember that case?”), while dozens of successful cases go unnoticed. What shouts loudest in memory is not the most frequent: it’s the most dramatic.

### **The trap of losing what you already have.**

Loss aversion. Kahneman and Tversky proved losing $100 hurts approximately twice as much as gaining $100 feels good. Same amount, asymmetric emotions.

Direct consequence for governance: change proposals are systematically rejected, even when the change is better than the current state. Because changing is perceived as loss (”we’ll lose what we have”), and staying is perceived as safety (”at least we know this one”). That’s not rational conservatism. It’s emotional bias dressed up as caution.

* * *

## **5\. Historical legacy.**

The impact of Kahneman and Tversky went beyond academia. Richard Thaler and Cass Sunstein used behavioral economics to create the concept of “nudge”: designing decision environments that compensate for biases without prohibiting anything. Governments in over 50 countries created “nudge units” to apply these ideas to public policy.

In 2011, Kahneman published _Thinking, Fast and Slow_ , which sold millions of copies and brought these concepts to dinner tables everywhere. When someone says “I thought too fast,” they’re using Kahneman without knowing it.

* * *

## **6\. Contradictions and limitations.**

Kahneman knew — and admitted — that knowing the biases doesn’t protect against them. “I know all the biases. I still fall for them,” he said in an interview. That raises an uncomfortable question: if even the expert can’t escape, what is the practical value of the discovery?

Kahneman’s answer was that individual solutions don’t work. We need structural solutions: change the decision environment, not the person. But there’s another weakness here: whoever designs the environment also has biases. Behavioral economics was criticized for assuming there exists someone neutral enough to “nudge” everyone else in the right direction. And several studies underpinning behavioral interventions failed to replicate in what became known as psychology’s “replication crisis.” The map of biases is robust. The solutions are still provisional.

## **7\. The translation into versioning.**

Kahneman died in March 2024, at 90, having never heard of Git or a repository. But what he discovered about the brain explains why versioning works better than most decision systems that exist.

An objection comes up often, and it’s the most honest one possible: “if even Kahneman admits that knowing biases doesn’t protect against them, what good is versioning? Whoever reviews also has confirmation bias. Whoever commits also anchors on bad numbers. The process suffers too.” The answer is the same one Kahneman gave: individual solutions don’t work. Structural solutions do. Versioning doesn’t fix anyone’s brain. It changes the environment in which decisions are made. That’s different, and it’s enough to reduce damage.

This article has no single anchor step in the grid. Its role is transversal: Kahneman justifies the logic behind several steps at once. Peer review (Step 12) compensates for confirmation bias because two brains with different blind spots cover each other. Fork (Step 18) resolves loss aversion without requiring the majority to accept change: whoever disagrees can test the alternative path without destroying the original. The audit trail (Step 14) anchors System 1 in recorded facts when memory distorts the past. And the minimum deadline before a vote, embedded in the logic of issues, gives System 2 the chance to wake up before System 1 has already decided.

**Peer review as bias compensation.** If I write a proposal (issue) and I approve it myself (merge), my biases pass right through. Anchoring, confirmation, availability: all intact. But if another person reviews it—with a different history, different shortcuts, and different blind spots—the chance that the error passes through decreases. Not because the reviewer is smarter. Because their biases are different from mine. Two brains bent in opposite directions come closer to straight than one bent brain alone.

**Fork as an antidote to loss aversion.** Loss aversion makes groups cling to bad decisions because changing seems risky. The fork solves this without drama: whoever disagrees doesn’t need to convince the majority—they can follow a parallel path and let the result speak. It’s the structural antidote to status quo bias.

**The log acts as a brake on System 1.** The complete history of decisions—who proposed, who reviewed, who approved, and what changed—is an anchor of reality. When someone says “we always did it this way” or “no one questioned that at the time,” the log responds with facts. Dates, names, recorded arguments. System 1 can distort memories. The log has no memory: it has a record.

A caution. Confirmation bias also attacks reviewers. A reviewer who already agrees with the proposal will look for reasons to approve, not to reject. Review is not a vaccine: it’s damage reduction. It works best when reviewers are diverse and independent — exactly the conditions that Surowiecki, in Article 15, will detail. If the reviewer group thinks alike, the review becomes a rubber stamp with two eyes instead of one.

## **8\. Practical implication.**

In 2008, the World Health Organization published a study on surgeries in eight hospitals across different countries, from Canada to Tanzania. The intervention was simple: a 19-item checklist to be followed before any surgical procedure. Confirm the patient’s name. Check the incision site. Check for allergies. Things any experienced surgeon “already knows.”

The result: serious complication rates fell 36% and deaths fell 47%. Not because surgeons became smarter. Because the decision environment changed: the checklist forced System 2 to participate at critical moments when System 1 habitually runs on autopilot. Kahneman didn’t design surgical checklists. But he explained exactly why they work: they don’t fix the brain—they change the conditions under which the brain operates.

Now imagine a homeowners’ association deciding whether to accept a construction company as a partner. The president presents the proposal in a meeting. Speaks first about the investment value (high anchor). Shows a photo of the company’s last project (availability: a nice image sticks). Asks who’s against it (loss aversion: no one wants to be the one who blocks progress). With versioning, the proposal is written and published in advance. Three different residents review it, each with access to the financial data, the company’s track record, and the contract terms. There’s a 72-hour window before the vote. System 2 has a chance to wake up. It doesn’t guarantee a perfect decision. It guarantees the decision wasn’t made by System 1 in a ten-minute impulse.

## **9\. Lessons learned.**

Knowing the bias doesn’t immunize. Structure compensates for what individual discipline cannot reach.

No one approves their own work because no one sees their own blind spot. The second pair of eyes exists for that.

The first number that enters the conversation contaminates the rest. Watch out for whoever speaks first.

Losing hurts more than gaining feels good. Every change proposal carries that disadvantage from the start.

System 1 decides in three seconds. If the decision matters, force System 2 to participate: deadline, written record, and review.

## **10\. Exploring paths and connections.**

The previous article (Lippmann) showed that information arrives in the brain already distorted: filtered by images, stereotypes, and narratives. This article went one level deeper: even if information arrived pure, the hardware that processes it has factory defects. Lippmann identified the problem at the input. Kahneman found the problem in the processor.

Part II began with Weber (the bureaucratic cage swallows the system), moved through Lippmann (reality arrives distorted), and now reached Kahneman (the brain distorts what arrives). Each layer reveals a different obstacle for any governance system. But one question remains: what happens when the volume of information is so large that the brain can’t even process it?

In 1989, an American information architect named Richard Saul Wurman coined a term that seemed like an exaggeration at the time and turned into prophecy: information anxiety. Wurman wasn’t concerned with biases or stereotypes. He was concerned with something more basic: what happens when so much information arrives that a person simply freezes? When excess doesn’t generate knowledge but paralysis.

If Kahneman showed that the processor is crooked, Wurman will show that the disk is full.
