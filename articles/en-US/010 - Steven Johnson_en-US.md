Collective intelligence does not need a coordinator. It needs simple rules, fast feedback, and diversity. An ant does not know that an anthill exists. Yet ants build cities. Your group can do the same—without needing an all-knowing leader. Johnson spent three years demonstrating exactly that.

### **The emergence of unplanned intelligence**

### **Article 07 of 21 in the series “Governance as Versioning.”**

* * *

 _This is Article 07. The thinker is Steven Johnson. The central Git concept is the repository as an emergent system—where Git concepts, “issue,” “merge,” “review,” and “fork” interact to produce collective intelligence no one planned. This article does not anchor to a single step in the 21-step sequence. Its role is different: to justify all 21 steps as an integrated system, not a checklist._

* * *

### **What will you find here?**

Have you ever been part of a group that worked better when the leader left the room? Or an organization that produced more when no one was actively coordinating?

That is not coincidence. It is emergence. And Johnson spent three years trying to understand why it happens.

* * *

### **1\. Where it all begins.**

New York, September 2001. Two weeks before the towers fell, a 32-year-old science writer handed the manuscript of a strangely titled book to Scribner: “Emergence.” The proposed subtitle on the cover is “The Connected Lives of Ants, Brains, Cities, and Software.” Ants, brains, cities, and software. In the same sentence. The editor must have thought Johnson had lost his mind—or discovered something.

He had discovered something.

Steven Johnson was not a biologist, neuroscientist, urban planner, or programmer. He was someone who read all of that simultaneously and saw connections that specialists, each trapped in their own corridor, could not see. He had spent three years obsessed with a question that sounds simple until you try to answer it: how do intelligent things arise from dumb parts?

A single ant is dumb. It follows half a dozen chemical rules. It does not know the anthill exists. But thousands of ants together build underground cities with ventilation, specialized chambers, and fungus farming. With no architect.

A single neuron is simple. It fires or it does not. But 86 billion neurons together generate consciousness. You, reading these words, are the proof.

A neighborhood resident makes local decisions. But millions of local decisions generate Chinatowns, financial districts, and bohemian zones. With no urban planner decreeing “this is where the arts district will be.”

Johnson called this phenomenon “emergence.” And he noticed that almost no one in politics was paying attention to it.

* * *

### **2\. Historical context**

The year 2001 was fascinating for anyone thinking about systems. The internet had just survived the dot-com bubble burst, and what remained standing were not the sites with the most money: they were the ones with the most people collaborating. Wikipedia launched in January of that year. Linux was already running on servers worldwide.

The dominant thinking, in both politics and management, was still vertical: leaders plan, subordinates execute. The idea that a system could be smarter than any individual participant sounded like mysticism. Johnson was about to show it was science.

* * *

### **3\. The central idea**

Johnson’s thesis fits in one sentence: when many simple agents follow simple rules and interact locally, a collective behavior emerges that none of them planned. That is emergence.

Think of a traffic jam. No driver decided to create gridlock. Each one braked a little; the one behind braked a little more; the effect propagated. The jam was designed by no one. It emerged from thousands of local decisions.

Before Johnson, most people (including scientists) explained complex systems from the top down. The anthill works because the queen commands. The city works because the mayor plans. The company works because the CEO decides. Johnson reversed the arrow. The queen does not command. The mayor does not plan Chinatown. The CEO does not design the company’s culture.

Intelligence is in the system, not in the leader.

This does not mean leaders are useless. It means most coordination happens without them, below the surface. And when a leader tries to control what should emerge, the result tends to be worse. Brasília was planned centimeter by centimeter. It is functional for cars and hostile to people. The cities no one designed (New York, Tokyo) are more chaotic and more alive.

* * *

### **4\. Developments**

### **The secret is the neighbor, not the map.**

Johnson identified four ingredients of emergence. First: agents need to interact locally. An ant does not see the anthill. It sees the three ants next to it. A neuron does not see the brain. It sees the neurons connected to it. A resident does not see the city. They see the block.

That limitation is the strength. Because local decisions respond to local information, which is the most precise and up-to-date available. Hayek (Article 3) called this dispersed knowledge. Johnson showed what happens when that knowledge connects: no central brain is needed. Intelligence emerges from the connections.

Google works this way. No human editor decides which sites are relevant. The algorithm looks at millions of local links (who cites whom) and extracts order. Relevance emerges. PageRank is applied emergence.

### **Feedback: the invisible engine**

The second ingredient is feedback. An ant finds food and leaves a chemical trail (pheromone). Other ants follow the trail. More ants on the same path strengthen it. The trail consolidates without anyone having drawn it. This is positive feedback: success attracts more success.

But when the food runs out, the pheromone evaporates. The trail disappears. This is negative feedback: failure corrects itself. Both together create a system that adapts without a coordinator.

Johnson saw in this a critique of traditional politics. A law is passed; results appear in years (slow feedback). Hard to reverse (blocked feedback). The system cannot self-regulate. It freezes.

### **Slime mold: organization on demand.**

Johnson’s favorite example was Dictyostelium, the slime mold. For most of its life, it consists of individual cells living independently. When food runs out, the cells aggregate, form a multicellular organism, and release spores to colonize new areas. With no leader, no plan. Once the problem passes, the organism dissolves.

Organization that emerges when necessary and dissolves when unnecessary. The opposite of permanent bureaucracy.

* * *

### **5\. Historical legacy**

“Emergence” was published in October 2001. The real influence came later, in waves. The generation of engineers who built Twitter, Reddit, and Stack Overflow read Johnson in the 2000s. The architecture of those platforms is emergent by design: content is not curated by editors—it is filtered by votes, shares, and local interactions.

In urban planning, the idea that cities function better when they grow from the bottom up influenced Jan Gehl, who redesigned Copenhagen for pedestrians following the logic of local interactions. In the software world, Johnson gave vocabulary to what open-source developers were already practicing. Linux had no name for what it did. After “Emergence,” it did: collective intelligence without central control.

* * *

### **6\. Contradictions and limitations**

Johnson knew emergence had limits but perhaps did not press that point hard enough. Emergence amplifies the rules of the system. If the rules are bad, the emergent result is catastrophe. The 2008 financial crisis was pure emergence: millions of agents following local incentives (easy mortgages, volume bonuses) generated systemic collapse. No one planned the crisis. It emerged. From the wrong rules.

There are also problems emergence does not solve. Going to the Moon required NASA, hierarchy, and centralized command. Not everything can be an anthill. The honest question is not “emergence or hierarchy?” but “when does each work best?” Johnson acknowledged this, but the book is so enthusiastic about emergence that the caveat stays in the background.

### **7\. The translation into versioning**

Johnson never used Git. “Emergence” was published four years before Torvalds created the system. What Johnson described as theory, Git implemented as code.

An objection appears here frequently: emergence works for ants and neurons, which have no legal obligations or contractual deadlines. A cooperative has commitments to suppliers. A city council has constitutional deadlines. An association has bylaws. I cannot let intelligence emerge—I need control. Johnson’s answer would be precise: emergence is not the absence of control. It is control in the right place. Not at the center, which lacks sufficient information. In the rules each agent follows locally. The 21 steps are exactly that: simple rules that, followed by each participant in their own context, generate coordination no central coordinator could produce.

This article has no single anchor step in the sequence. Its role is different: to justify the 21 steps as an integrated system, not a list of procedures. Johnson explains why simple rules generate complex results. Each step in isolation seems small (open an issue, make a commit, request a review). The system of 21 steps together is what no single step could produce: traceability, distributed correction, and legitimacy through process. Emergence is not in each step. It is in the interaction between them. In particular, Johnson justifies the feedback between Issue (Step 9) and Merge (Step 13), which makes the system learn without a coordinator; the distribution of Review power (Step 12), which avoids the queen-who-commands bottleneck; and Fork (Step 18) as necessary selective pressure—the threat that keeps the main system honest.

**A Git repository is an emergent system.** Thousands of contributors (autonomous agents) follow simple rules (commit, review, merge, fork). Each works locally, in their own branch, seeing only their piece of the problem. No one needs to see the entire project. Feedback is fast: a pull request approved or rejected in days, sometimes hours. Control is distributed: anyone can fork.

The result is smarter than any single participant. Linux has millions of lines of code that no person fully understands. Quality emerges from interaction, not from a plan.

**The issue is a local signal.** When someone opens an issue, they are reporting information from their corner of the system. It is the ant that found food and left pheromone. If many issues appear in the same module, the system is saying where the problem is. No one needs to coordinate centrally. The pattern emerges from local signals.

**Merge is positive feedback.** Good code is accepted. More people use it. More contributions arrive. The module strengthens. A revert is negative feedback: bad code is removed, and the trail weakens. Together, they make the repository self-regulate—exactly as the anthill redirects ants to the food source that is producing results.

But the vigilance warning matters. If someone monopolizes the merge (only one person approves code), the emergence dies. You have recreated the queen-who-commands; the anthill CEO that Johnson showed does not exist. Git works because review power is distributed. Concentrating review kills emergence. It is returning to the cathedral when the bazaar has already proven it works.

### **8\. Practical implication**

Luis von Ahn and SETI@home: emergence without voice

In 2000, UC Berkeley launched SETI@home: a program anyone could install on their computer that used idle processing power (when the machine was not doing anything) to analyze radio signals from space in search of extraterrestrial life. No coordination between participants. No meetings. No voting. More than five million computers across 226 countries contributed fragments of processing to a problem no central supercomputer would have solved alone. The project ran for 21 years, analyzed more than 1.7 million signals, and shut down in 2020.

A few years later, Luis von Ahn, a Guatemalan researcher at Carnegie Mellon, invented reCAPTCHA. You know those distorted letters you decipher to prove you are not a robot? Von Ahn realized that every time someone solved a CAPTCHA, they were doing work computers could not do: recognizing old handwritten text. He redirected it: while you prove you are human, you are digitizing pages from 19th-century books that no recognition software could read. More than 750 million people helped digitize the archive of The New York Times and Google Books without knowing it. He then created Duolingo with the same logic: learn a language while translating the internet.

Both cases are emergence in its purest form: millions of autonomous agents, simple rules, and a collective result no central planner would have produced. Johnson would have recognized the pattern immediately.

But there is a difference that matters for governance: SETI@home and reCAPTCHA are emergence without deliberation. Participants contribute without proposing, reviewing, or approving. They work for problems with a verifiable answer: the signal was there or it was not, the letter is this or it is not. They do not work for collective decisions where the process matters as much as the result. A homeowners’ association cannot digitize a shared-space policy without residents knowing they are doing it.

What governance as versioning adds to Von Ahn’s emergence is the political layer: a commit has authorship, a pull request has deliberation, and a merge has a record. Intelligence still emerges bottom-up. But each contributor knows what they are contributing, and the group can see, contest, and revert any part of the result.

In 2010, Japanese researchers published an experiment that baffled transportation engineers. They placed a fungus called “Physarum polycephalum”—a single-celled organism with no brain, no neurons, and no central processing structure—on a map of Japan. They positioned oat flakes at the locations of the main cities around Tokyo and observed.

In 26 hours, the fungus had created a network of filaments connecting all the cities. The network was efficient, redundant, and resistant to failure. It was, with small variations, the map of the Tokyo subway system, which took decades and billions of dollars to design by human engineers. The fungus planned nothing. It followed simple rules: grow toward nutrients, reinforce paths that work, and abandon those that do not. Johnson would call it emergence. The engineers called it embarrassing. Intelligence was not in the fungus. It was in the rules it followed.

An illustrative example: a homeowners’ association with 200 families does not need a president who decides everything. It needs simple rules (written proposals, peer review, public record), local communication channels (groups by block, not a full assembly for every problem), and fast feedback (decision tested, result measured, adjustment made). The collective intelligence of 200 families will outperform any elected board. Not because they are geniuses, but because there are many of them and they see different angles.

### **9\. Lessons learned**

Collective intelligence does not need a leader. It needs rules, feedback, and diversity of agents. If the system has all three, intelligence emerges.

Local interaction beats central planning in complex environments. The person closest to the problem has the best information.

Fast feedback is the engine. Without feedback, there is no adaptation. Without adaptation, there is no emergence. The system freezes.

Emergence is not magic. It is a consequence of rules. Bad rules generate destructive emergence. Designing the right rules is the central task of any open system.

Permanent bureaucracy is the opposite of emergence. The slime mold teaches: organize when needed, dissolve when not needed.

* * *

### **10\. Exploring paths and connections.**

Johnson closes the circle of Part I: when diverse agents interact with simple rules and feedback, collective intelligence emerges without anyone having designed it. The skeleton of why decentralization works is assembled. But every system has a dark side.

In 1922, a German sociologist published a monumental work called “Economy and Society.” Max Weber had a disturbing observation about organizations: every bureaucracy, however well it starts, tends to become a cage. The rules that existed to serve the people begin to exist to serve themselves. Process becomes more important than result. Rational on the outside, irrational on the inside.

Weber called this the “iron cage.” And if the 21 steps of “Governance as Versioning” become an empty ritual (commit done just to tick a box, review no one reads, automatic merge), the cage closes again.

Part II begins with that warning. And it begins by asking: what prevents an open system from consuming itself?
