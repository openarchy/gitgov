### The Woman Who Went to See the Pasture

### **Article 12 of 21 of the Governance as Versioning series**

* * *

This article covers Elinor Ostrom (1933–2012), economist and Nobel laureate. The central Git concept is tag / release (Step 19 of 21). Ostrom showed that self-organized communities have managed shared resources for centuries—without a boss and without an owner—using eight principles that map, with surprising precision, onto what makes an open-source project work.

* * *

#### What will you find here?

Have you ever been part of a group that managed something collectively—a garden, a shared space, a common fund—and watched it work without a boss or an owner?  
Mainstream economics said that was impossible. Ostrom went to check.

* * *

## 1\. Where it all begins

Bloomington, Indiana, 1990. A 57-year-old woman hands her editor the manuscript that will dismantle one of the most repeated beliefs in modern economics. Her name is Elinor Ostrom. The book is called _Governing the Commons_. The thesis fits in one sentence: communities can manage shared resources without needing government or an owner.

It sounds obvious. It wasn’t.

For more than twenty years, economists repeated a mantra: if a resource belongs to everyone, no one takes care of it. Garrett Hardin published “The Tragedy of the Commons” in 1968 and convinced the academic world that shared resources always collapse. The logic: each shepherd puts one more sheep on the common pasture. The profit is his. The cost of degradation is shared by everyone. Everyone puts in too many sheep; the pasture dies.

A conclusion that became dogma: either the government controls it, or someone privatizes it. A third way does not exist.

Ostrom heard the argument, found it elegant, and did something Hardin never did. She went to check. She traveled to villages in Switzerland, irrigation systems in Spain, fisheries in Maine, and forests in Nepal. She collected data for decades. She interviewed hundreds of real communities, with real people, managing real resources.

And she found that Hardin was wrong. Not in theory: in reality.

Self-organized communities have been managing commons (shared goods: any resource that belongs to the group, not to an individual) for centuries. Without state bureaucracy. Without privatization. With rules created by those who use the resource, mutual monitoring, and sanctions ranging from a warning to banishment. In 2009, she won the Nobel Prize in Economics. First woman. Not for elegant theory. For field evidence.

* * *

## 2\. Historical context

The twentieth century offered two answers for organizing collective life: the state or the market. The left defended central planning. The right defended private property. The debate was binary, and each side was absolutely certain the other was wrong.

Hardin fit the Tragedy of the Commons into that framework. The theory was used to justify the privatization of water in Africa, the enclosure of communal lands in India, and fishing regulations imposed from the capital on communities that had fished for generations.

No one asked the communities whether they needed help.

Ostrom came from political science, not theoretical economics. She worked with field data, not with Blackboard models. When the economist said, “Rational individuals destroy shared resources,” she replied, “Show me where.”

* * *

## 3\. The central idea

Ostrom’s argument is devastating in its simplicity: Hardin theorized. I went to see.

And when she went to see, she found the opposite of what the theory predicted. Communities managing shared resources successfully. Not for a year or a decade. For centuries.

Think of the shared space in your apartment building. The rooftop grill is a commons. If every resident uses it without rules, someone leaves it dirty, someone monopolizes it on the weekend, someone breaks it, and no one fixes it. Tragedy of the Commons in miniature. Hardin’s solution: either the building manager controls it with an iron fist (state) or someone buys the grill and charges rent (privatization).

Ostrom would say, “Or the residents organize themselves. They create usage rules, decide together what happens to whoever breaks them, and adjust the rules when the situation changes. No authoritarian manager. No grill owner.”

Before Ostrom, mainstream economic science said this was naive. After Ostrom, it became published empirical evidence.

* * *

## 4\. Developments

### The eight screws that hold the shelf together

Ostrom did not stop at observation. She compared hundreds of cases — successes and failures — and extracted eight principles common to groups that work. They are not commandments. They are patterns that appear where things go right and are absent where things collapse.

**First: clear boundaries.** Who can use the resource? In Törbel, Switzerland, communal pasture has functioned for 500 years because “resident” has a precise definition. An outsider does not use it.

**Second: rules adapted to the local context.** Irrigation in Valencia, Spain, has different rules from irrigation in Nepal. Both work because they were written by those who know the ground, not by those who saw the map.

**Third: those who are affected participate in creating the rules.**

**Fourth: monitoring carried out by the users themselves, not by an inspector who has never set foot in the place.**

**Fifth: graduated sanctions** (warning, light fine, heavy fine, expulsion — in that order, without skipping a step).

**Sixth: fast and local conflict resolution.** The Tribunal de les Aigües in Valencia has met every Thursday for a thousand years; any farmer brings their grievance, the judgment is public, and the decision is immediate.

**Seventh: the external government recognizes the legitimacy of local rules and does not override them.**

**Eighth: layered governance.** A small canal managed by a few farmers; a medium canal coordinates the small ones; the main canal governs the entire basin.

### When the commons breaks down

Ostrom never said the commons always work. She said they can work under specific conditions. And she studied failures with the same rigor.

Commons collapse when change arrives too fast (industrial boats destroyed artisanal fisheries that had worked for generations), when the government invalidates local rules (European colonizers replaced centuries-old irrigation systems with generic regulations), when the resource becomes a high-value global commodity, or when scale exceeds the capacity for mutual monitoring.

A village of 200 people, where everyone knows each other: it works. A city of two million: mutual monitoring is unviable without additional tools. Ostrom knew that scale was the limit. She died in 2012, before seeing what happens when that limit is overcome by technology.

* * *

## 5\. Historical legacy

The 2009 Nobel was not a courtesy. It was belated recognition of decades of field work that contradicted orthodoxy. The Royal Swedish Academy admitted what economists were reluctant to accept: the third way exists.

The impact was concrete. Forest communities in India and Nepal gained academic backing to defend their management systems against privatization pressure. International organizations began including “community governance” as a real option in development projects. The United Nations Convention on Biological Diversity incorporated principles of local management based on Ostrom’s work.

Less visible, but relevant: the eight principles became the reference for those trying to understand why some communities cooperate and others do not. From agricultural cooperatives in Brazil to free software communities, Ostrom’s principles appear—sometimes without anyone knowing where they came from.

Ostrom listed 8 principles of commons that work. Saint Benedict practiced all of them 1,400 years earlier, without knowing the vocabulary. The Rule of Saint Benedict is the oldest and most enduring empirical case of a well-governed commons in the Western world.

Fifteen centuries before Ostrom systematized the principles, Saint Benedict of Nursia was writing the Rule that would practice them for more than a thousand years. Each monastery is autonomous with local rules, an abbot elected by the community, peer monitoring, graduated sanctions, and layered governance. The work of the copyists was a versioning protocol: a verified copy, an error corrected in the next version, and the original preserved. It was not democracy. It was a commons with deliberate design. Ostrom found the principles by studying real communities. Benedict discovered them by building one.

* * *

## 6\. Contradictions and limitations

Ostrom studied relatively small and stable communities. Most of her classic cases involve groups of hundreds, at most thousands, of people with long-term relationships. It works when everyone knows each other. When the scale grows to millions, the mutual monitoring that sustains the system becomes difficult without additional structures that Ostrom did not detail.

There is also a tension in the eighth principle. Layered governance can easily become the bureaucracy that Weber described in Article 8. The upper layer begins to serve itself. Ostrom acknowledged the risk but did not offer a specific mechanism to prevent it. She trusted that communities would resolve it. Sometimes they trust too much.

## 7\. The translation into versioning

Ostrom never saw a Git repository. She died three years before GitHub reached ten million users. But her eight principles describe, with surprising precision, what makes an open-source project work.

An objection appears here in two flavors. From the right: “Self-organized community is nice in medieval Swiss villages; it doesn’t work at a modern scale.” From the left: “Your eight principles assume good faith, but they don’t prevent political capture by a strong leadership that uses the process to consolidate power.” Both objections are legitimate. Ostrom’s answer to the first is already in the data: she documented commons that work at significant scale when the eight principles are present. The answer to the second is that versioning adds something that pastures and fisheries did not have: the system does not depend on anyone’s good faith. It depends on whoever acts in bad faith, making that behavior visible and recorded. Deviation from the eight principles is documented in the log. The political cost of deviating in public is higher than deviating in private. It does not eliminate the problem. It changes the incentive equation.

**Step 19—Tag / Release:** In Ostrom’s commons, there are moments when the group stops, evaluates the complete cycle, and ratifies that the rules remain valid. The renewal of the pasture-use agreement. The assembly of fishers confirms quotas for the next season. These moments have a specific function: turning accumulated practice into an explicit decision, making visible what worked and what needs to change. In the versioning system, Tag and Release do exactly that. A tag marks the point at which a version was validated by the group and is stable enough to be referenced. A release is the collective recognition: this version works, we trust it, it is the starting point for the next cycle. It is not perfect. It is good enough for the whole group to use. Ostrom would call it legitimacy earned through practice. The process does not impose the version. The group recognizes it.

**README is the first principle** (clear boundaries and rules adapted to context). The README says who can contribute, how to contribute, and what type of contribution is accepted. Each project defines its own. Linux has different rules from Debian, which has different rules from WordPress. There is no universal model, and that is why it works: the rules reflect who uses it.

**Review is the fourth principle** (monitoring by the users themselves). No outsider audits. The collaborators themselves review each other’s work. Whoever understands the code evaluates the code. Ostrom discovered this in fishing villages. Torvalds implemented it in software without knowing it.

**Fork is the seventh principle** (recognized autonomy). If the group disagrees with the direction of the project, it copies everything and goes another way. No one has veto power over dissent. In Ostrom’s commons, communities that disagreed with regional management could create their own arrangements. In Git, “fork” is a button.

But pay attention. A repository can have a perfect README, active review, and fork available and still become a closed club. If technical or linguistic access bars participation (Castells will explore this in Article 18), Ostrom’s principles exist on paper, but not in practice. A digital commons that only works for those who know English and programming is not a commons: it is a cathedral with a bazaar façade.

## 8\. Practical implication

In 1989, the city of Porto Alegre launched participatory budgeting—a system by which residents directly decided where part of the municipal budget would be invested. Neighborhood assemblies, elected delegates, and publicly voted priorities. By 1995, the program reached 14,000 direct participants and had reversed the historical logic of investment concentration in the wealthiest neighborhoods: the majority of resources began going to peripheral areas, where needs were greater and voices, until then, smaller.

What Ostrom would have recognized immediately: all eight principles were present. Clear boundaries (neighborhood resident, not lobbyist). Rules adapted to local context (each region elected different priorities). Participation by those affected. Monitoring by the residents themselves. Graduated sanctions for delegates who did not show up. Local dispute resolution. Recognition of city hall as the instance that executes, not that decides. And layers: neighborhood, district, city. It was not utopia. It was Ostrom’s principles applied without anyone knowing her name.

Take a residents’ association managing a community garden. The eight principles are a checklist. Who can use it? (boundaries). What are the planting and harvesting rules? (local rules). Who decides to change the rules? (participation). Who verifies that they are being followed? (monitoring). What happens when someone breaks them? (graduated sanction: conversation, warning, suspension). How do you resolve a dispute about who planted what? (conflict resolution). Does city hall respect the internal rules? (autonomy). And if the garden grows to several neighborhoods? (layers). If any of those eight is missing, the garden will have a problem. Not maybe. It will.

* * *

## 9\. Lessons learned

The third way exists. Neither state nor market: community. It is not utopia. It is field evidence.

Rules imposed from outside generate compliance at most. Rules created by those who use them generate commitment.

Monitoring works when those who monitor know the context. An outside inspector is expensive, slow, and easily circumvented.

Sanctions that start at maximum destroy cooperation. First warning, then fine, then exclusion. In that order.

If the rule cannot be adjusted by those affected, the system is not open. It is a cathedral with a plaza’s name.

* * *

## 10\. Exploring paths and connections

Ostrom is the first prescription of Part II: when the people who use the resource define the rules, the system works. Not perfectly. But it works. For centuries. Previous articles stacked up diagnoses of failure. Ostrom showed that there is a way out.

What remains to understand is the engine. The eight principles describe what works. They do not explain why. What makes a system correct itself when something goes off track? What makes another system ignore the signals and collapse?

In 2008, an American environmental scientist named Donella Meadows published (posthumously) a slim book that answered that question with one word: feedback. The result of an action that returns and influences the next one. A thermostat that turns off the heater when the temperature reaches the right point. Brakes that reduce speed when the car approaches a wall. Or a bubble that inflates until it bursts because no one hears the signal to stop.

Ostrom showed what works. Meadows will show the invisible mechanism that makes it work: the loop that connects action, result, and correction. And she will show what happens when that loop is too slow — or when someone turns it off.
