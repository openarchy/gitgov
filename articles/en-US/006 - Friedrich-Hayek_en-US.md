Have you ever made a perfect decision on paper that failed in practice? Hayek explained why in 1945: the knowledge that matters is not at the decision-maker's table. 

This article shows the connection and what it has to do with any group that needs to coordinate.

### **The knowledge no one holds completely.**

#### **Article 03 of 21 in the series “Governance as Versioning”.**

* * *

_This is Article 03 of the Governance as Versioning series. It covers Friedrich Hayek, the Austrian economist who explained in 1945 why no central planner can ever have enough information to make good decisions for everyone else. The Git concept at the center of this article is Issue. This corresponds to Step 9 of the 21 Steps: registering a problem as a visible, traceable signal._

* * *

**What you’ll find here.**

Have you ever been in a meeting where someone decided for everyone without knowing what each person actually needed? A manager who set the procurement policy without asking the people who would use it? A committee that approved the budget without understanding each department’s real situation? The decision looked perfect on paper. In practice, it served no one.

That is not a problem of bad intentions. It is a problem of information. And Hayek was the first to explain why it is unsolvable if you depend on a single head to make all the decisions.

* * *

## **1\. Where it all begins.**

London, September 1945. The war ended four months ago. Europe is a map of rubble. Entire cities turned to dust. And in university corridors, in cafés, in newspapers, one question dominates everything: how do we rebuild?

The most popular answer was simple: planning. If we managed to plan the Normandy landing — 150,000 soldiers in a single morning — why not plan the economy? If a committee of generals coordinated the largest military operation in history, why couldn’t a committee of economists coordinate the production of shoes, wheat, and steel?

Friedrich August von Hayek, an Austrian exile of 46 living in London, published that month a 20-page article in the American Economic Review. No equations. No charts. The thesis fit in one paragraph: no planner, however brilliant, can gather the knowledge needed to make good decisions for everyone. Because the knowledge that matters is not in any central office. It is scattered across the minds of millions of people, and each one knows things no one else knows.

Most intellectuals of the time ignored the article. Central planning was the consensus. Hayek was the inconvenient one.

It took decades to discover that the inconvenient one was right.

* * *

## **2\. Historical context**

The world of 1945 loved planning. The Soviet Union, which helped defeat Hitler, operated with a planned economy: the State decides what to produce, how much, where, for whom. It seemed to work (at least from a distance). Post-war England created the welfare state with heavy government intervention. Even the United States had just come out of Roosevelt’s New Deal, with the government intervening heavily in the economy.

The dominant idea: if you can organize an army, you can organize an economy. All it takes is enough intelligence, data, and power.

Hayek saw a problem in that logic. Not a problem of degree (we need more data), but of nature (the kind of knowledge required cannot be centralized). He wasn’t saying central planning was difficult. He was saying it was impossible.

* * *

## **3\. The central idea**

Hayek’s thesis begins with a correction. Most people think the economic problem is: “we have resources — how do we distribute them?” Hayek says the real problem is different: “who knows where each resource is most needed?”

Think of a baker. He knows how many loaves he sells per day. He knows that on Mondays he sells less because the neighborhood empties on weekends. He knows that when it rains, foot traffic drops. He knows that his flour supplier raised prices last week. That knowledge lives in his head. It is not in a report in Washington. It is not in a government spreadsheet. It is right there, at that counter, on that corner.

Hayek called this “knowledge of the particular circumstances of time and place.” It is not scientific knowledge, not theory, not statistical data. It is local, practical knowledge that changes by the hour. The farmer knows which patch of his land has buried rock. The cab driver knows which street is closed. The shop owner knows that the neighbor shut down and her customers will need another supplier.

Now multiply that by millions of people, decisions, and contexts. How can a central committee in Moscow — or Washington — know all of that?

Answer: it cannot. Even with the best computers, the data would be outdated before it reached the desk. Reality changes faster than any bureaucracy can process.

## **4\. Developments**

### **The thermometer no one invented.**

If no one has the complete picture, how do millions of strangers coordinate their actions? Hayek found the answer in a mechanism no one designed: prices.

Prices are signals. When oil becomes scarce, the price rises. You don’t need to know there was a crisis in the Middle East. The price tells you. Companies cut consumption. Consumers take the bus. Inventors start researching alternatives. All without anyone giving an order.

Each person adjusts in the way that makes most sense to them. The cab driver (who depends on the car) keeps driving. The executive (who can work from home) stops going to the office. Millions of individual decisions, each using local knowledge, coordinated by a single number: the price. It is like a thermometer no one invented, but everyone reads.

### **The mistake no one fixes.**

In a market economy, when someone makes a mistake, the cost is theirs. They stop, change strategy, try something else. The error is local and the correction is fast. When a central planner makes a mistake, the error belongs to everyone. And the correction takes years, because the one who made the error is the same one who should correct it. There is no immediate feedback. There is no competition showing that a better alternative exists.

The Soviet Union produced shoes in enormous quantities. But in the wrong sizes, the wrong styles, in the wrong cities. The factories met their numerical targets. The people walked barefoot. The number on the report said success. Reality said failure. And no one on the central committee knew, because feedback was blocked.

### **Freedom as a precondition**

A year before the article on knowledge, Hayek published _The Road to Serfdom_. The thesis was simple: if the State controls the economy, it controls your life. Whoever decides where you work, how much you earn, what you produce, decides whether you eat. And whoever decides whether you eat, decides whether you speak.

Hayek was not defending savage capitalism. He was delivering a warning: total control of the economy leads to total control of politics. Not because of the malice of rulers, but because of the logic of the system. If every economic decision passes through a committee, dissent becomes sabotage. Contrary opinion becomes a crime. The path is gradual. And by the time you notice, the cage has already closed.

## **5\. Historical legacy**

The Soviet Union lasted 74 years and collapsed in 1991. Endless lines, chronic scarcity, black markets on every corner. Cuba, North Korea: the pattern repeats. Central planning produces beautiful reports and empty shelves.

Hayek won the Nobel Prize in Economics in 1974. Margaret Thatcher carried _The Road to Serfdom_ in her handbag and slapped the book on the table in Parliament. The fall of the Berlin Wall in 1989 was the empirical confirmation of what Hayek wrote in 1945.

* * *

## **6\. Contradictions and limitations**

Hayek overestimated the market’s reliability as a coordination mechanism. Markets work poorly when there are monopolies, externalities (pollution, for example), or extreme information asymmetry. The person selling a used car knows the engine is failing. The buyer does not. The price there is not aggregating knowledge; it is hiding it.

And Hayek underestimated the coordinating power that computers and networks would bring. He was right that a committee in Moscow couldn’t process millions of data points. But the internet can. It did not resolve the problem he identified (tacit knowledge remains local), but it changed the scale of what is possible.

## **7\. The translation into versioning**

Hayek died in 1992, before the internet exploded. He never saw a Git repository. But his ideas explain with surgical precision why open governance works better than centralized governance.

An objection appears here frequently: Hayek’s argument is about markets and prices. What does that have to do with a cooperative, a city council, a residents’ association? Everything. The mechanism is the same: when those who decide are separated from those who feel the consequences, the knowledge that matters never reaches the table. The way to fix that is not to find a smarter manager. It is to design the system so that knowledge from the edges reaches the center before any decision is made.

In a closed system (Raymond’s cathedral), a small committee decides everything. That is Hayek’s central planner: a few brains trying to process more information than they can handle. In an open system (the bazaar), thousands of people contribute what they know. Each one sees a different piece of the problem. A programmer in India finds a bug no one in California would see. A developer in Brazil notices that the interface doesn’t work for right-to-left readers. Dispersed knowledge, each person contributing from their particular circumstances of time and place. Hayek would recognize this immediately.

**Step 9 — Issue:** an issue is Hayek’s “knowledge of the particular circumstances of time and place” in the form of a written proposal. When a member of a cooperative registers a problem — wrong input, a deadline that doesn’t work, a decision that damages their specific reality — they are doing exactly what the market does with prices: transforming local information into a visible signal for decision-makers. An open issue is a signal of a problem. A resolved issue is a signal of correction. The accumulation of issues in one area is a signal of systemic instability. The system generates distributed information that no central coordinator needs to interpret alone.

Hayek’s prices find their parallel in other versioning signals. The number of reverts in one area is a signal of instability (a nervous market). The volume of forks is a signal of dissatisfaction with the official version (consumers migrating). The system generates information that no board meeting could produce.

And the vigilance alert: even in an open repository, it is possible to create a bottleneck. If one person controls the merge, they have become the central planner. If issues pass through a committee that takes weeks to respond, feedback is blocked. An open system can reproduce every defect Hayek identified in central planning, if permissions are designed wrong. Lessig would say: code defines what is possible. Hayek would say: if information doesn’t flow, the system dies.

## **8\. Practical implication**

In 1988, economists at the University of Iowa created a simple experiment: a market where anyone could bet real money on the outcome of American elections. No researchers, no expert panel, no sampling methodology. Just bets, with real money, from anyone who wanted to participate.

The result unsettled election research specialists. In presidential elections from 1988 to 2004, the Iowa Electronic Markets outperformed traditional opinion polls in accuracy in 74% of cases. The explanation is Hayekian: those who bet real money use everything they know, including what researchers cannot capture in a sample. The neighbor who noticed yard signs on the street, the shopkeeper who saw a drop in sales of the candidate’s merchandise, the union official who sensed declining enthusiasm at the base. Dispersed knowledge, impossible to centralize, that the market price aggregates in real time. No committee of experts managed to gather what thousands of bettors with real stakes in the correct answer gathered automatically.

Now imagine: an agricultural cooperative in rural Paraná decides that all input purchases will be centralized in a three-person committee. Result: the committee buys fertilizer that works well for soybeans, but half the members grow corn. The committee didn’t know, because they never asked. In a versioned system, each farmer opens an issue with what they need. The committee sees the full picture before deciding. Not because it got smarter, but because the system made dispersed knowledge flow.

* * *

## **9\. Lessons learned**

No one has the complete picture. Design systems that work with incomplete information, not systems that wait for perfect information.

Centralizing decisions wastes most of what people know. The system needs to listen to the edges, not the top.

Slow feedback kills. If those who make mistakes don’t discover quickly that they erred, the mistake grows. Fast revert is more valuable than perfect planning.

The human bottleneck is the new central planner. If one person controls the merge, you have rebuilt Moscow with a better interface.

## **10\. Exploring paths and connections.**

The introduction showed the tools (Stallman, Raymond, Torvalds). Locke answered where authority comes from: consent. Popper answered what to do when the system errs: correct fast, without violence. Hayek answered who has the information to correct: no one alone, everyone together.

Three articles, three foundations stacked on each other. Consent. Correction. Dispersed knowledge.

But one piece is missing. If no one controls everything and knowledge is scattered, how do things work? How do millions of people, without coordinating anything, produce something that looks like it was planned?

In 1949, an Austrian economist (who happened to be Hayek’s teacher) published a 900-page book called _Human Action_. Ludwig von Mises had an answer to that question. And the answer was unsettling: order does not need an organizer. It emerges on its own.

That is called spontaneous order. And if you understand that concept, you will understand why Wikipedia works without an editor-in-chief, and why the language you are reading right now works without anyone having designed it.
