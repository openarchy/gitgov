### **Deciding on a world that doesn’t exist.**

#### **Article 09 of 21 in the series “Governance as Versioning.”**

 _This is Article 09 of the Governance as Versioning series. The thinker is Walter Lippmann. The central Git concept is FETCH—retrieving information from a source without automatically merging it. This corresponds to Step 14 — Transparency / Audit trail._

* * *

#### **What will you find here?**

Have you ever voted on something without reading the original document? Have you ever approved a decision based on a summary someone else prepared? Have you ever had a firm opinion about a problem you never experienced directly?

Lippmann studied this. And the conclusion was more uncomfortable than anyone expected.

* * *

## **1\. Where it all begins.**

New York, autumn of 1918. The war is over. In the streets, there were flags, confetti, and people crying with relief. But in a government office, a 28-year-old journalist sits before a pile of newspaper clippings, military reports, and diplomatic telegrams. His face doesn’t match the celebration outside.

In the previous two years, Walter Lippmann had worked in the American propaganda machine. He helped draft Woodrow Wilson’s Fourteen Points. He participated in the Versailles Peace Conference. He watched, from a front-row seat, how governments manufacture versions of reality and distribute them as if they were facts.

And he realized something he couldn’t shake: American public opinion about the war had been built on false, exaggerated, or selective information. Newspapers described German soldiers as barbaric baby-eaters. Posters portrayed the Kaiser as a demonic monster. The population hated Germany with an intensity that corresponded to nothing they had actually experienced. No one there had been in a trench. They had read about trenches. Seen photographs. Heard accounts. What they knew was an edited version, distilled through dozens of intermediaries between the fact and the reader.

What disturbed Lippmann was not the existence of propaganda. It was realizing that this was not an exception: it was the rule. Not only in wartime. Always. About everything.

Four years later, in 1922, he published _Public Opinion_. The book that dismantled the illusion that citizens decide based on reality.

* * *

## **2\. Historical context**

The early 1920s lived a contradiction. On one side, democracy seemed to triumph: empires fell, republics were born, voting expanded. On the other, the First World War had proven that entire populations could be led to slaughter by fabricated narratives.

The dominant thinking was optimistic: democracy works because informed citizens make good decisions. All that was needed was a free press and public education.

Lippmann looked at that belief and said, “Beautiful, but false”—not because people are foolish. Because the task is impossible.

* * *

## **3\. The central idea**

Lippmann’s thesis fits in one sentence: people do not react to the real world. They react to simplified images they carry in their heads.

He called these images a “pseudo-environment“—pseudo because they seem real but aren’t. Environment, because it is in this fabricated world that you live, decide, and vote.

Think of the last time you formed an opinion about a crisis in a country you’ve never visited. You didn’t see the crisis. You didn’t talk to anyone who lives there. You read a news article (written by someone who may also not have been there), watched a thirty-second video (edited by someone with an agenda), and heard a commentator (who has fifteen minutes to explain what historians spend years understanding). And from that, you have a firm opinion.

Before Lippmann, the assumption was this: give citizens information, and they will decide well. After Lippmann, the question changed: what information? Filtered by whom? Simplified how? Framed for what?

The practical consequence is uncomfortable. When a condo association votes in favor of a renovation, are the residents deciding based on the actual project (which no one read to the end) or on the two-paragraph summary the property manager sent through the WhatsApp group? When a board approves a policy, do the board members see the raw data or the twenty-slide presentation prepared by someone who already had the conclusion ready?

Almost always the second option. And it’s not an accident. It’s structure.

* * *

## **4\. Developments**

### **The map that eats the territory.**

Lippmann opened the book with an example that never left people’s minds. A Pacific island, 1914. English, French, and German residents living peacefully together. News arrived by ship once a week. In August, World War I broke out in Europe. But the next ship hadn’t arrived yet. For weeks, the English and Germans continued playing cards together.

When the news arrived, they became enemies. Behavior changed not because reality changed (the war already existed), but because the image in their heads changed.

This happens every day. A company is doing well, but the newspaper publishes a negative story: stocks plunge. The neighborhood is safe, but a violent crime appears in the news: residents lock their doors. The map you carry in your head determines your decisions more than the real territory. And someone drew that map for you.

### **The stereotype as a habit-forming shortcut.**

Lippmann popularized the word “stereotype” in the sense we use today. Stereotypes are mental shortcuts for dealing with a world that is too complex. You don’t evaluate each person individually (expensive, slow). You apply a ready-made category: “all politicians are corrupt,” “civil servants don’t work,” and “startups are always innovative.”

The problem is not that they exist. It’s that they resist contrary evidence. If you believe group X is incompetent and you meet someone from X who is brilliant, your brain doesn’t update the stereotype. It classifies them as an exception. The shortcut reinforces itself with each use, like a trail in the woods that grows wider the more people walk it.

### **The manufacture of consent**

Lippmann coined an expression that Noam Chomsky and Edward Herman made famous decades later: “manufacturing consent.” The idea is simple and uncomfortable: in modern democracies, governments do not govern by force. They govern by shaping what people think they know.

It doesn’t need to be a conspiracy. Journalists work under tight deadlines and depend on official sources. Editors choose headlines that sell. Press offices frame narratives before any reporter arrives. The result is a system in which public opinion is constructed before it is consulted. The assembly votes yes. But the options were already designed by whoever controls the framing.

* * *

## **5\. Historical legacy**

The impact of _Public Opinion_ was concrete. The book inspired the creation of the first communications programs in the United States. The Frankfurt School (Adorno, Horkheimer) developed its critiques of the culture industry from premises that Lippmann had articulated first.

In politics, the consequence was twofold. Governments understood and exploited the mechanism: the propaganda that Lippmann analyzed became the model for all subsequent government communications. Researchers began to question the journalism profession itself. The concept of “agenda setting”—the media doesn’t tell you what to think, but what to think about—is a direct descendant of Lippmann’s. The expression “manufacturing consent” became the title of Chomsky and Herman’s 1988 book, which dissected how major American media outlets filtered information in favor of corporate interests.

Eighty years later, South Korean philosopher Byung-Chul Han updated Lippmann’s diagnosis for the digital era in _Infocracy_ (2022). Lippmann’s pseudo-environment was built by newspapers, radio, and press offices—identifiable intermediaries with deadlines and agendas.

Han showed that the contemporary intermediary is invisible: the algorithm that decides what you see before you know you are being filtered. The difference is not one of degree. It is one of architecture. Lippmann described a world where the map was drawn by others. Han described a world where you don’t know you’re using a map.

* * *

## **6\. Contradictions and limitations**

Lippmann diagnosed the problem with surgical precision. The solution he proposed, not so much. He advocated for an enlightened technocracy: neutral experts would analyze data and produce objective reports, and citizens would choose between leaders, not between policies. Government for the people, not by the people.

The blind spot is obvious: who watches the experts? Bank economists recommend policies that benefit banks. Lippmann’s technocracy presupposes neutrality where no neutrality exists. And he underestimated the capacity for collective learning: he had no way of foreseeing that millions of coordinated amateurs (Wikipedia, Linux) would produce knowledge that could rival any elite.

## **7\. The translation into versioning**

Lippmann died in 1974. He never saw a personal computer and never heard of Git. But the problem he described — the pseudo-environment — is the ghost that haunts every governance system.

An objection comes up frequently: “Versioning solves Lippmann’s problem because it records everything. With the full log available, anyone can verify reality; you’re no longer dependent on the intermediary.” It’s a serious objection. And an incomplete one. Transparency is a necessary condition, not a sufficient one. If the log exists but no one reads past the summary, the intermediary stays in control. The real difference is that versioning transforms the pseudo-environment from a monopoly into a choice: whoever wants to verify can. Before, they couldn’t. Lippmann didn’t ask everyone to read the original document. He asked that the original document exist and be accessible. The versioning system solves exactly that part.

**Step 14—Transparency / audit trail:** The complete record of who proposed what, when, with what justification; who reviewed it; and who approved it is the structural antidote to Lippmann’s pseudo-environment. An audit trail—the auditable record of all decisions—breaks the monopoly of whoever controls the summary: at any time, any participant can reconstruct the decision chain from raw data, without depending on another’s edited version. It doesn’t guarantee that everyone will do this. It guarantees that anyone can. That possibility changes the behavior of whoever writes the summaries: knowing the original is visible discourages deliberate distortion. Transparency doesn’t eliminate the pseudo-environment. It raises the cost of manipulating it.

Peer review is the second line of defense. If I propose a change based on my map (incomplete), another reviewer brings theirs (incomplete in a different way). Two incomplete maps overlap, and the area covered grows. Five reviewers with different perspectives assemble a mosaic that none of them could see alone. Lippmann would say this doesn’t eliminate the pseudo-environment. He would say it reduces distortion. And he would be right.

A well-written issue is a practical antidote to the stereotype. “We need to change the process because it’s bureaucratic” is a stereotype. “Proposal 47 took 23 days because it sat with reviewer C for 18 days without a response” is information. The system forces the translation of stereotypes into verifiable data.

The vigilance warning is serious: whoever writes the README defines the group’s pseudo-environment. Whoever drafts the issue summary determines how reviewers will read the proposal. Lippmann showed that intermediaries are inevitable. The question the system must answer constantly is, “Are these intermediaries reducing distortion or increasing it?”

## **8\. Practical implication**

In March 2003, the United States invaded Iraq on the claim that the country possessed weapons of mass destruction. The justification was presented to the UN Security Council with satellite photographs, technical diagrams, and intelligence reports. It appeared to be solid evidence. It was a pseudo-environment assembled from real documents. Five years later, the Senate Select Committee on Intelligence confirmed: the assessments were based on unverified assumptions, questionable sources, and analyses that confirmed conclusions already drawn. American public opinion had supported the war at rates above 70% in the first months. Not because they saw Iraq. Because they saw the presentation of Iraq. The map was mistaken for the territory, and the cost was measured in hundreds of thousands of lives.

If you participate in any group that makes collective decisions, Lippmann’s lesson changes one thing: distrust the summary. Whenever possible, go to the raw data. When someone presents a problem, ask: “How do you know this?” When you feel you have a firm opinion about something you haven’t experienced, stop. Ask yourself: Am I reacting to the fact or to the image of the fact?

The system cannot force anyone to think better. But it can be built so that the raw data is always accessible alongside the summary.

* * *

## **9\. Lessons learned**

An opinion about what you haven’t seen is a bet, not a certainty. Treat it as a bet.

Whoever controls the summary controls the decision. Make sure the summary never exists without a link to the original.

Peer review doesn’t eliminate distortion. It reduces it. It’s the difference between driving blindfolded and driving with a dirty windshield.

Transparency without accessibility is decoration. If the data is open but no one can read it, the openness is fictional.

The best governors are not the most informed. They are those who know they are misinformed and build systems to compensate for it.

* * *

## **10\. Exploring paths and connections.**

Weber showed the internal enemy: the bureaucracy that ossifies. Lippmann revealed the external enemy: the distorted information that feeds the system. Together, they form the oldest and most current pair of warnings in this series. The machine can function and produce bad results if the people operating it are deciding on a world that only exists in their heads.

Part II is about limits. Internal (Weber), external (Lippmann). But there is a third, deeper limit. It is not the system that fails. It is not the information that arrives distorted. It is the hardware that processes the information.

We are in 1979. In Jerusalem, two Israeli psychologists are about to publish a paper that will change what we know about human decision-making. Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky will prove, through controlled experiments, that the brain is not a rational machine that occasionally makes mistakes. It is a shortcut machine that makes mistakes in predictable ways. Anchoring. Availability. Confirmation. These are not occasional bugs. They are the operating system.

Lippmann showed that the image of the world arrives distorted. Kahneman will show that even if it arrived perfectly, the brain would distort it again.
