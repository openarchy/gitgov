“That’s how it’s always been done” is not an argument. It’s a parasitic meme. Richard Dawkins wasn’t talking about funny images on the internet. He was describing how any idea competes, mutates, and survives—or doesn’t. The concept changes how you read a proposal, a policy, or a homeowners’ association rule. Absurd rules don’t persist because they work. They persist because nobody spends the energy to challenge them.

Bad ideas beat good ideas because the criterion isn't truth. It's contagion. Biology and Git say the same thing about bad ideas.

### **Why do bad ideas survive?**

#### **Article 06 of 21 in the “Governance as Versioning” series.**

* * *

_This is Article 06 of 21 in the Governance as Versioning series. The thinker is Richard Dawkins. The central Git concept is merge (Step 13): the selection filter that determines which ideas enter the official version—not because they are true, but because they survived the process._

* * *

**What will you find here?**

Have you ever wondered why certain absurd rules keep existing even when everyone knows they don’t work? Why does a useless procedure survive ten different administrations? Why is “that’s how it’s always been done” enough of an answer to shut down any debate?

Dawkins found the answer. And it’s anything but comforting.

* * *

## **1\. Where it all begins.**

Oxford, 1976. A 35-year-old biologist delivers to his editor a manuscript with a provocative title: _The Selfish Gene_. Richard Dawkins wasn’t famous. He was an assistant professor—one step above nobody in the British academic hierarchy.

The book made a claim that unsettled even biologists sympathetic to evolution: you are not the protagonist of your own story. Your genes are. You are a temporary vehicle, a body built to carry molecules that have been copying themselves for billions of years. When you die, the body goes. Genes stay, jumping to the next generation.

It sounds cold. It is cold. But it explained animal behavior with a precision no previous theory had reached.

The real gut punch, though, came in the last chapter. Dawkins asked a question nobody expected in a biology book: what if genes aren’t the only thing that copies itself? What if ideas also replicate, jumping from brain to brain, competing with each other, surviving not because they are true, but because they are good at being copied?

Dawkins needed a name for this unit of cultural replication. He wanted something that sounded like “gene.” He thought of “mimeme” (from the Greek _mimesis_ , imitation). He shortened it. It became a “meme.” A four-letter word that, forty years later, became the most ironic thing on the planet: the word “meme” became a meme.

* * *

## **2\. Historical context**

In 1976, biology was living through a silent civil war. The tradition explained evolution as something that benefits the species: animals cooperate “for the good of the group.” But why do worker ants toil until death without reproducing? Why do bees sacrifice themselves when they sting?

The traditional answer was vague: “for the good of the species.” Dawkins, alongside William Hamilton and George Williams, said no. The worker ant shares 75% of its genes with its sisters. By protecting the nest, it protects copies of its own genes. The calculation belongs to the gene, not the individual.

This shift in perspective — from organism to gene as the unit of selection — was the revolution. And the last chapter extended the logic into territory no biologist had ever set foot in: human culture.

* * *

## **3\. The central idea**

Dawkins’s thesis fits in two sentences: genes replicate through bodies, and ideas replicate through minds. And the criterion for survival, in both cases, is not quality. It is copyability.

Think of your grandmother’s carrot cake recipe. She taught it to your mother. Your mother taught it to you. The recipe travels from mind to mind, changing a little with each jump (your mother adds chocolate; you drop the sugar), and the versions people repeat most often survive. Not because they are the most nutritious. Because they are the most copied.

That is a meme: a unit of cultural information that replicates from brain to brain. Melodies, slogans, superstitions, recipes, jokes, bureaucratic rules. Anything transmitted by imitation or repetition.

The problem is that meme selection does not filter for truth. It filters for contagion.

“That’s how it’s always been done” is the most resilient meme on the planet. Not because it’s useful, but because it requires no argument. The person who questions it must spend energy; the person who repeats it doesn’t. The meme parasitizes the host’s cognitive laziness and spreads effortlessly. An absurd rule in a homeowners’ association, a company, or a government can survive intact for decades — not because it works, but because nobody spends the energy to challenge it. The meme won. Reason lost.

Before Dawkins, it was assumed that traditions persisted because they were wise. After Dawkins, it became clear that many persist because they are contagious. Wisdom is optional.

* * *

## **4\. Developments**

### **The memeplex: why packages beat loose pieces.**

Memes rarely travel alone. They travel in packages that Dawkins called memeplexes. Religion is an example: belief, rituals, morality, promise of eternal life, and punishment for nonbelievers. Each piece reinforces the others. To question one is to question the entire package.

Political ideologies work the same way. If you declare yourself “right-wing” or “left-wing,” you buy a package: positions on economics, customs, and foreign policy, all at once. Disagreeing with one item risks expulsion from the tribe. The memeplex protects itself by demanding loyalty to the whole, not piece-by-piece evaluation.

### **The difference between a parasitic meme and a symbiotic meme**

Not every meme harms those who carry it. “Wash your hands before eating” saved millions of lives. It replicates well (simple, practical) and helps the host. Dawkins would call this a symbiotic meme.

A parasitic meme is the opposite: it spreads well and harms the carrier. “There’s no point voting—the system is corrupt anyway” replicates easily (confirms cynicism, requires no effort) but weakens democracy.

The distinction is essential: the problem isn’t that memes exist. It’s that the environment determines which ones survive. Without filters, parasites dominate. With selective pressure for functionality (review, evidence, testing), symbionts have the advantage.

### **Science as artificial selection of memes**

Dawkins saw in science the best example of artificial selection applied to ideas. Paper published (proposal). Peer review (test). Replication of the experiment (verification). If the meme holds up, it strengthens. If it fails, it dies. It’s not perfect: science has biases, fraud, and fads. But it is the system with the most honest selective pressure humanity has ever invented.

* * *

## **5\. Historical legacy**

 _The Selfish Gene_ sold more than one million copies in over 25 languages. The word “meme” entered the Oxford English Dictionary in 1998. The irony: the academic concept was swallowed by the popular version. For most people, “meme” means a funny image on the internet, not a unit of cultural replication.

But the original concept changed entire fields. Daniel Dennett used memes to explain consciousness. Susan Blackmore built a complete theory of memetics. Every study on virality, disinformation, or “infodemic” owes something to the last chapter of a 1976 biology book. Platforms like Reddit, Wikipedia, and Stack Overflow are meme selection machines: proposals compete, votes filter, and the most useful versions rise.

* * *

## **6\. Contradictions and limitations**

Dawkins was accused — with partial justification — of imprecision. “Meme” never acquired a definition as rigorous as “gene.” Genes have a chemical structure (DNA), a precise copying mechanism, and a measurable mutation rate. Memes have none of that. Where does a meme begin and end? Is “wash your hands” one meme or two (”wash” and “your hands”)? The answer varies depending on who’s asking.

Dawkins also underestimated the role of context. Genes operate in a predictable biochemical environment. Memes operate in human brains, which are anything but predictable. The same meme can thrive in one culture and die in another. The gene-meme analogy works as a metaphor but stumbles as exact science. Even great intuitions have limits, and memetics never consolidated itself as an autonomous discipline.

## **7\. The translation into versioning**

Dawkins never saw a Git repository. But governance as versioning is, without exaggeration, a meme selection machine.

An objection comes up here often: “meme” has become synonymous with internet jokes. Dawkins’s original concept is cultural biology, not viral humor. What does that have to do with board decisions, cooperative votes, or public deliberation? Everything. Every proposal that enters a governance system is a meme competing for survival. The system doesn’t judge whether the proposal is good. It judges whether it passes the filters. Dawkins is describing exactly what happens when an idea enters circulation: it mutates, competes, survives, or dies. Versioning is the environment that determines which memes win.

Every open issue is a meme competing for attention. Someone proposes a change: the meme enters the environment. The community evaluates. If the idea replicates (others support it and refine it), it gains traction. If not, it dies in the queue.

Peer review is selective pressure. When a reviewer points out flaws, they are testing the fitness of the meme. Poorly grounded proposals are filtered out before entering the official version. The system creates an environment where bad memes have shorter lives.

**Step 13 — Merge:** Merge is survival confirmed by the collective filter. The meme passed through review, was evaluated by other pairs of eyes, withstood objections, and entered the official repository. Not because someone declared it true or perfect. Because it survived the selective pressure of the process. Dawkins would see in merge the exact equivalent of natural selection: what enters MAIN is not the prettiest or the most elaborate. It’s what the environment judged to be fit. And if the meme that seemed fit turns out to be parasitic after the merge? The revert comes. Selection doesn’t stop at approval.

A fork is the weapon against memeplexes. When the dominant package refuses mutation, someone copies the repository and takes a different path. Cultural evolution through speciation: same origin, different trajectories. The fork doesn’t destroy the original. It tests an alternative. If it’s more fit, it attracts contributors. If not, it dies. Natural selection of entire systems.

The vigilance alert is the institutional memeplex. “We’ve always done it this way” is the most dangerous meme inside any governance system. If the 21 steps become an unquestioned ritual (commits out of obligation, reviews nobody reads), the system has turned into a self-protecting memeplex. When the process protects itself more than it protects the result, Weber’s iron cage (Article 8) has already closed.

## **8\. Practical implication**

In September 1970, an American economist published an article in _The New York Times Magazine_ with a one-line thesis: the only social responsibility of a business is to increase its shareholders’ profits. The idea was simple, easy to repeat, and solved a real problem for executives: it justified cutting anything that didn’t generate immediate return.

The meme replicated with the speed Dawkins would have recognized. Within twenty years, it had become doctrine in business schools, boardrooms, and executive incentive contracts. In 2008, the global financial system collapsed — partly because banks had optimized short-term shareholder returns while accumulating invisible risk on their balance sheets. The idea wasn’t true. It was replicable. And replicable ideas survive even when they produce bad results, because selection acts on the capacity to spread, not on the quality of the content. The meme won. The system lost.

A neighborhood association that decides “we always hold our meetings in person on Tuesdays” is carrying a meme. It might be a good meme (in-person builds trust) or a parasite (it excludes people who work nights). The difference between a healthy system and a fossilized one is simple: is the meme revisable? Can someone open an issue questioning the format? If the answer is “no, that’s how it’s always been done,” the meme won. The association lost.

## **9\. Lessons learned**

Ideas survive by being contagious, not by being true. Be suspicious of anything “everyone knows” but nobody questions.

“That’s how it’s always been done” is a warning sign, not an argument. If the only defense of a rule is its age, the rule needs a review.

Environments without filters select parasites. If any proposal enters without review, the loudest ones dominate—not the most useful.

Identity is not an argument. When criticizing an idea becomes attacking a person, the debate is dead, and the memeplex has won.

The antidote to bad memes is not censorship: it’s selective pressure. Transparency, peer review, and public records create an environment where lying is expensive and correcting is cheap.

## **10\. Exploring paths and connections.**

This is the sixth article in the core series. Locke showed that authority comes from consent. Popper, that the value of a system lies in the speed of error correction. Hayek, that knowledge is dispersed. Mises, that order emerges without an organizer. Shannon, that information is what reduces uncertainty. Dawkins completed the picture with an uncomfortable question: what if the ideas circulating in the system are selected not for quality, but for contagion capacity?

Six pieces fitted together. One is still missing. Because every article so far has dealt with isolated agents: genes compete, memes replicate, and individuals act. But what happens when millions of simple agents, following simple rules, interact at the same time?

In 2001, Steven Johnson published a book that looked at ants, neurons, and cities and saw the same thing in all three. No ant knows what the anthill is doing. No neuron knows what the brain is thinking. No resident planned the neighborhood where they live. And yet the anthill functions, the brain thinks, and the neighborhood specializes.

Johnson called this emergence. If memes compete at the individual level, emergence is what happens at the level of the entire system. Where does the intelligence that nobody planned come from?
