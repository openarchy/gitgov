# Ludwig von Mises
## The order nobody designed.
### Article 04 of 21 of the series "Governance as Versioning."

This is Article 04 of the series Governance as Versioning. It covers Ludwig von Mises and the concept of spontaneous order — the idea that coordination emerges from individual action without any central planner.   

In the 21 Steps framework, this article anchors Step 7 (MAIN): the official version of the group's decisions is not decreed by anyone. It emerges from the distributed selection process itself.

### What will you find here?
Have you ever tried to organize a group that worked well without a board meeting, without internal bylaws, without anyone really in charge? And wondered how that was even possible.  

Mises spent his entire life explaining that phenomenon. And the answer he found was so simple that most people refused to believe it.

### 1. Where it all begins
New York, autumn of 1949. An Austrian man of 67 delivers to his editor a manuscript of nine hundred pages. No equations. No graphs. Not a single table. The title: Human Action.
Ludwig von Mises was not naive. He knew the book would land in a hostile environment. John Maynard Keynes ruled the universities. The consensus was clear: governments must plan, intervene, regulate. Economists who disagreed were relics. Mises was the most stubborn of them.  

He had fled Vienna in 1934, four years before the Nazis annexed Austria. He went to Geneva, then to New York, at 58, without a steady job, without fluent English. New York University offered him an unpaid position. A private foundation covered the cost. His colleagues tolerated him. The establishment ignored him.
Human Action sold poorly. The reviews were cold.  

He died in 1973, at 92, convinced he had failed.
He was wrong. About the failure, not the theory. Because the theory, silently, had already conquered the world. Not through universities. Through practice. Every system that works without a central boss — Linux, Wikipedia, the Portuguese language, the price of a tomato at the market — works exactly as Mises described. Order without an organizer. Coordination without a coordinator. Complexity born of simplicity.

### 2. Historical context
The world of 1949 was a world of planners. World War II had ended four years earlier. Governments had won the war with central planning: rationing, production control, industrial direction. The lesson seemed obvious: if central planning defeated Hitler, it can defeat poverty.  

The Soviet Union, with its five-year plans, presented itself as an alternative. In Europe, governments were nationalizing railways, mines, energy. The dominant question was not "should the government plan?" but "how much?"  

Mises answered: zero. Not because planning was difficult. Because it was impossible. Not for lack of will. For lack of information.  

Hayek, in the previous article, showed that knowledge is dispersed. Mises took the next step: if no one has complete information, how does order arise? Answer: order does not need to be created. It emerges.

### 3. The central idea
Mises's thesis fits in one sentence that sounds absurd on first reading: order does not need an organizer.  

Think about the English language. No one sat in a room and decided what "serendipity" would mean. No one planned the verb conjugations. No one designed grammar before people started speaking. Language arose from use. Millions of people, over centuries, adjusted sounds, meanings, rules. No coordinator. No committee. No decree.  

The result is a communication system so sophisticated that no linguist can design an equivalent from scratch. Planned languages (Esperanto) function. Languages that emerged from use live.  

Mises called this spontaneous order. Not anarchy. Not chaos. Coordination born of the actions of millions of individuals, each pursuing their own goals with their own information, without anyone having designed the whole.  

The price of a tomato at the farmers' market is spontaneous order. No minister decided it would cost a dollar fifty. The price emerges from the interaction between the grower, the transporter, and the buyer. Each one knows things the others do not. The price aggregates everything into a single number.
Before Mises, most people assumed that order requires someone in charge. After Mises, it became clear: many of the most complex things in the world were not designed by anyone. They were built by everyone.

### 4. Developments
The axiom no one can deny.
Mises built his entire economics on one foundation: humans act. It seems trivial. It is not. To act means:  

(1) to be dissatisfied with something,  
(2) to imagine a better state,  
(3) to believe that your action can change reality,  
(4) to choose between alternatives, giving up what you did not choose.  

Try to deny it. Denying is an action. You just proved what you tried to refute. The axiom is irrefutable because any attempt at refutation confirms it.  

This foundation allows something powerful: if every action involves choice, observing what people do reveals what they value. Not what they say they value: what they do. Mises called this demonstrated preference. The politician who claims to defend austerity and votes himself a raise has demonstrated his real preference. The number of open issues in a repository demonstrates what participants consider a problem. Words inform intention. Actions inform truth.
The thermometer no planner can replace.  

If order emerges from individual actions, how do people coordinate without talking to each other? Through prices. Mises saw prices as signals carrying compressed information about the world. When the price of steel rises, the message is: steel became scarcer, use less or find an alternative. No one needs to know why. The signal is enough.  

Without prices, no calculation. Mises proved this in 1920: in an economy where the State owns everything, there are no real prices for means of production. Without prices, the planner cannot know whether he is using steel for bridges when he should be using it for hospitals. He guesses. And guessing with the lives of millions has a name: tragedy.
In the digital world, equivalent signals exist. Stars on GitHub, downloads, forks, number of contributors: these are "prices" that say where the value is. No central committee needs to decide which project deserves attention. People decide by acting. Order emerges.

### 5. Historical legacy
Mises wrote his argument about economic calculation in 1920. The Soviet Union spent more than seventy years trying to prove him wrong. When it fell in 1991, the archives revealed: factories producing shoes no one wanted, steel mills wasting more resources than they generated, wheat rotting in warehouses while people lined up for bread.
Hayek, Mises's student in Vienna, took the professor's ideas to London and Chicago. Rothbard radicalized the Austrian school. Kirzner developed the theory of the entrepreneur as discoverer of opportunities. The Austrian school influenced the reforms of Thatcher and Reagan. Not because they read Mises. Because the ideas had spread through think tanks and advisors. Memes, in Dawkins's sense: ideas that replicate.
Asimov imagined in 1942 what Mises described in prose: a system no one controls, but that produces order across generations, as long as the initial conditions are well designed.  

Why the Foundation is central, not peripheral.
Asimov wrote the Foundation series in the 1940s, directly inspired by Gibbon's Decline and Fall of the Roman Empire. Hari Seldon's question is not generic science fiction. It is the same question Benedict of Nursia asked in 529 AD: when the central system collapses, what preserves accumulated knowledge?  

The protagonist Seldon's answer is protocol. Not heroism, not charismatic leadership. A distributed repository with geographic redundancy (two Foundations at opposite ends of the galaxy), with future version plans already embedded in the design, and with a correction mechanism built in (the Seldon Plan that adjusts to deviations).
That is Git. Literally.  

And psychohistory has a structural limitation that Asimov acknowledged: it works for masses, fails for exceptional individuals. The character called "the Mule" breaks the entire plan because he is an outlier the model did not anticipate. This is exactly Taleb's argument about fragility: a system optimized for the expected collapses before the unexpected.  

The Foundation is not led by Seldon. It is the repository he left for those who came after.

### 6. Contradictions and limitations
Mises had a blind spot the size of his own theory. If spontaneous order is so powerful, why do real markets produce monopolies, pollution, financial crises? The Austrian answer (it is always the fault of government intervention) is elegant but partial. Markets without any regulation can generate concentrations of power as dangerous as any government.  

Mises also underestimated non-market cooperation. He insisted that rational action is always individual and utilitarian. But people contribute to free software without monetary gain, donate blood, build Wikipedia. Human motivation is more complex than "everyone pursues their own interest." Spontaneous order works. But it works for reasons Mises did not fully capture.

### 7. The translation into versioning
Mises never saw a computer. He died eighteen years before Torvalds created Linux. But his concepts describe with surgical precision what happens in an open repository.
An objection appears here frequently: spontaneous order works in the market, where everyone acts out of self-interest. But a cooperative, a council, an association has rules, hierarchy and accountability. I cannot let everything emerge on its own.  

Mises's answer would be direct: you do not let everything emerge without rules. You design simple rules and let the result emerge from the process. The difference between a committee that decides what everyone needs and a system that aggregates what each person reports is not the absence of structure. It is where structure operates: in the process, not in the result.
Linux is digital spontaneous order. No one planned the result. Thousands of people, each with their own skills and time, contributed what they knew. The result is more sophisticated than any company could have designed. No CEO. Just process.  

Step 7 — MAIN: in the 21 Steps system, MAIN is the official branch (rama) — the document where validated group decisions live. The wrong intuition is imagining that someone decreed what goes into MAIN. The correct intuition — Mises's — is that MAIN emerges from the process: it is the accumulated result of all contributions that passed review (revisión) and were accepted. No one designed the MAIN. It is what remained after the distributed selection process worked. Spontaneous order with clear entry rules.  

Open issues (incidencias) are demonstrated preference. When someone opens an issue, they are saying through action that something needs to change. The volume of issues on a topic is a price signal: it shows where dissatisfaction is. No one needs to run a survey. The system aggregates the information on its own.  

Merge (fusión) and revert (reversión) are profit and loss. Accepted code (merge) is the equivalent of profit: the contribution generated value and was absorbed by the system. Rejected or reverted code is loss: the attempt failed, the system signals "this does not work here." Without both signals, there is no learning. A repository that accepts everything without review is like an economy without prices: it seems to work, but accumulates invisible waste until it collapses.  

Fork (bifurcación) is competition. Mises said competition prevents monopoly. In Git, fork does the same: if the original project becomes rigid or captured, anyone can copy everything and go their own way. The threat of a fork keeps the project honest.  

Vigilance alert: if someone monopolizes the merge, they have become a central planner. If a small group decides which issues are "valid" without transparent criteria, they have become a Soviet committee. Centralizing signals kills the system from within.

### 8. Practical implication
In 1974, two American engineers, Vint Cerf and Bob Kahn, published a technical paper describing a communication protocol between computers. The central idea was radical: no central computer would control traffic. Each data packet would find its own path through the network, choosing available routes in real time. No one planned, no one coordinated, no one knew in advance where the information would travel.  

Mises would say it was spontaneous order in the form of protocol. The internet has no owner, no headquarters, no director. It has simple rules that, followed by billions of independent devices, produce a network that moves quintillions of bytes per day. No central planner could have designed this. The complexity emerged from the simplicity of the rules, exactly as Mises described for market prices: it is not the result of anyone's plan, but it works better than any plan could work.  

To illustrate: imagine a cooperative of farmers in the Brazilian interior that needs to decide the planting calendar. Cathedral option: the president decides alone. Mises option: each farmer records what they observe on their lot (pests, rainfall, soil, local prices). No one knows everything. Everyone knows something. The aggregate of those observations, visible on a shared board, produces a decision more intelligent than any president could make alone. No genius in charge is needed. What is needed is a system that aggregates what each person knows.

### 9. Lessons learned
Order does not need an organizer. It needs simple rules, clear signals, and freedom to act.  

What people do reveals more than what they say. Watch actions, not speeches.  

Without feedback signals (prices, reviews, metrics), no system learns. Accepting everything is as destructive as rejecting everything.  

Centralizing decisions means wasting what each person knows. Decentralizing means trusting the process.  

The threat of competition (fork) is what keeps any system honest.

### 10. Exploring paths and connections
This is the fourth article in the core series. Locke established where authority comes from: consent. Popper showed that the value of a system is not getting things right, but correcting them. Hayek proved that no central brain has enough information to decide for everyone. Mises completed the foundation: if no one controls everything, order arises from the actions of those who participate. No planner. No boss. No vote.  

Four pieces in place. But there is one question none of the four answered: what exactly is the information that flows through these systems?  

Hayek said prices carry information. Mises said actions reveal preferences. But no one defined information as a precise, measurable concept with a formula.  

In 1948, an engineer at Bell Laboratories published 55 pages that changed the world without the world noticing. Claude Shannon did something that seems impossible: he measured information. He gave it a unit (the bit), a formula, and a definition that dismantles centuries of confusion: information is not what you know. It is what reduces your uncertainty.  

If you think you know what information is, get ready to discover you do not.
